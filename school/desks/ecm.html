5 The sub-desks: open each one

ECM is 5 products sharing one syndicate desk. Open each panel for the mechanics, the math, and where deals die.

IPO execution the flagship

Six months of manufacturing a public company, compressed into a 2-week sprint at the end. The syndicate's product is a stable aftermarket: price the deal so it works for the issuer on day 1 and for the buyer at month 6.

The sequence
  1. Org meeting to first filing (8 to 12 weeks). S-1 drafting, audits, governance cleanup; bake-off decided the league table months earlier.
  2. Testing the waters. Confidential meetings with 15 to 30 anchor accounts before any public filing; their feedback sets the frame.
  3. Launch and roadshow (8 to 10 days). Public range set; management does 60+ meetings; the book builds in real time: who, at what limit, at what size.
  4. Pricing day. The syndicate allocates: long-onlies anchored, hedge funds rationed, flippers cut. Greenshoe (15% over-allotment) sold short at pricing to stabilize the aftermarket.
  5. Aftermarket. 25-day quiet period, then research initiations; 180-day lockup, then the follow-on calendar begins.
The math
The IPO discount: price at roughly a 10 to 15% discount to fair value so the deal trades up. A book "20x covered" is mostly inflated limits: the syndicate reads quality (who holds at 6 months) not multiple. Gross spread ~7% on small IPOs, 1.5 to 3.5% at mega-cap, split 20/60/20 management fee/selling concession/underwriting.
What goes wrong
  • Pricing through the range then breaking issue: the deal that "worked" for 1 day poisons the issuer's next 3 trades.
  • Windows close fast: a 15% VIX week shuts the calendar; deals that hesitate re-file next quarter.
Follow-ons & block trades the risk business

Once public, every subsequent share sale is a speed-vs-price trade. The modern market has collapsed toward overnight risk: the bank buys the stock from the seller at a discount and owns the exit problem.

The menu
  • Marketed follow-on: 2 to 4 days of marketing; best price, most leak risk.
  • Overnight bookbuild (agency): launch post-close, price pre-open; the bank takes no position.
  • Block trade (risk): banks bid after the close for the whole stake, 2 to 6% below last sale, and resell into the open. The bid is a competition: sellers run 30-minute auctions among 3 to 5 desks.
  • Wall-crossing: sounding out 5 to 10 accounts under NDA pre-launch to de-risk the print; the compliance tightrope of the whole business.
The math
A $1B block bought at a 4% discount needs to clear above the purchase price across maybe 2 trading days: the desk's P&L is (resale VWAP less purchase price) x shares, less hedging slippage. One misjudged print (bought at 4% off, market wants 8%) erases a quarter of block profits, which is why the bid sheet prices liquidity (ADV multiples), borrow availability, and event risk, not just the chart.
What goes wrong
  • Sponsor cleanup fatigue: the 4th drip from the same holder trades wider each time; the market front-runs known overhangs.
  • Leaked wall-crosses: the stock fades before launch and the trade is dead, plus the regulator calls.
Convertibles equity with a bond costume

A bond plus an embedded call option on the issuer's stock. Issuers sell vol they think is cheap and buy cash coupons they think are expensive; convert arb funds buy the package, short the delta, and trade the gamma.

The terms that matter
  • Coupon: 0 to 3%, far below the issuer's straight-debt cost.
  • Conversion premium: 25 to 40% above the reference price; the strike of the embedded option.
  • Call protection, soft calls (130% trigger), and settlement flexibility (cash, shares, or combo) drive the accounting and dilution story.
  • Call spread overlays: issuer buys a call spread to push effective dilution to a 75 to 100% premium; costs 8 to 12% of proceeds.
The math
Price = straight bond value + option value. The desk quotes it in implied vol: a convert issued at 38 implied when realized runs 45 is cheap to the arb, expensive to the issuer, and it will fly. Rate cycles run the calendar: when straight debt costs 9%, a 1.5% coupon convert with 30% premium is the CFO's easiest meeting of the year.
What goes wrong
  • Dilution denial: boards that treat the premium as "free" meet the share count again at 130% of strike.
  • Hedge flow feedback: heavy arb ownership means mechanical selling into rallies and buying into selloffs, which management never enjoys learning about live.
Rights issues & TERP math the recap tool

The European (and distressed) workhorse: existing holders get tradeable rights to buy new shares at a deep discount, pro rata. Nobody is diluted who participates; the discount is optics, not cost.

The math
TERP = (market cap + proceeds) / (old + new shares). Stock at 10, 1-for-4 rights at 6: TERP = (4x10 + 1x6)/5 = 9.20. The "40% discount" headline is really a 35% discount to TERP; the right itself is worth TERP minus subscription price (3.20). Underwriters price their fee (1.5 to 3%) against the risk the stock falls through the subscription price during the 2 to 3 week window.
When it appears

Balance-sheet repair (banks post-2008, travel post-2020), funding a jumbo acquisition, or the endgame of a leverage problem. In the US the same job is usually done with a marketed follow-on or a PIPE; in the UK, pre-emption rights make the rights issue the default above ~10% dilution.

What goes wrong
  • Falling through the subscription price: the rights go worthless, the underwriters own the rump, and the fee was never enough.
  • Rump placement leaks: the unsubscribed block overhangs the close.
Private placements & PIPEs the negotiated print

One buyer (or a handful), one negotiation, no roadshow. Speed and certainty priced against a discount and structure: the tool for companies that cannot or will not run a public process.

The spectrum
  • Common-stock PIPE: 5 to 15% discount, registration rights; the clean version.
  • Structured PIPE: convertible preferred with dividends, ratchets, or resets; the term sheet reads friendlier than it settles.
  • Late-stage privates / pre-IPO rounds: the crossover funds (Fidelity, T. Rowe, Tiger class) buying ahead of the public window.
  • Registered directs and ATMs: the drip alternatives for small caps.
The math
Price a structured PIPE by decomposing: face + dividend stream + conversion option + downside protections. A "20% premium convert preferred with a 2x liquidation preference" can carry a true cost of capital north of 15% while the press release says 6%. The desk's job, on either side, is to compute the settlement distribution, not the headline.
What goes wrong
  • Death-spiral resets: ratchets that reprice on every downtick manufacture the dilution they insure against.
  • Signaling: a PIPE at a discount tells the market the public window was closed; the stock often gaps to the PIPE price and keeps going.
