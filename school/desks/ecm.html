ECM.

Equity capital markets prices companies in public: IPOs, follow-ons, block trades, and convertibles. It is the desk where valuation stops being a deck and becomes an order book, and where the market grades your work by 9:31 the next morning.

D.1 The primer

ECM sits between issuers and public investors: it advises on when the window is open, builds the story, runs the roadshow, and allocates the book. The product is a cleared price with a healthy aftermarket: priced too high, the deal breaks and the client's stock is damaged; too low, the client left money on the table.

The flow business: IPOs get the headlines, but follow-ons and blocks pay the bills: a block trade is ECM at its purest: the desk bids risk on a chunk of stock at 6pm and resells it overnight. Converts boom whenever rates are high (issuers rent cheaper coupons by selling upside). Sponsor exits (IPOs then drip follow-ons) tie the desk to the PE cycle; the IPO window itself opens and closes with volatility: above ~25 on the VIX, almost nothing prices.

D.4 The landscape

Bookrunning is a bulge-bracket oligopoly: Goldman, Morgan Stanley, and JPMorgan lead global ECM league tables, with BofA and the Europeans in the flow; boutiques appear as independent advisers on IPOs (a growing niche: advising the issuer about the banks). Across the table: long-only anchors (Fidelity, T. Rowe, Capital) whose orders make books, hedge funds who flip, and index funds who must buy later: allocation is a game about exactly that. The buy-side counterpart career is equity syndicate and IPO-focused investing.

D.5 The study kit

Process end to end (org meeting to greenshoe exercise), the last 5 significant IPOs: range, price, first-day, where they trade now: and one converts term sheet read until the math is friendly. ECM interviews are market-feel interviews: current windows, recent deals, and honest talk about why a deal worked or broke.
