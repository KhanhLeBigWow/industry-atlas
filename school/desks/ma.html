5 The sub-desks: open each one

M&A is several distinct crafts wearing one name. Open each panel for the process, the math, and the failure modes.

Sell-side auctions the bread and butter

Selling a company is process engineering: manufacture competitive tension, protect confidentiality, and keep 2 credible bidders at the table until signatures. The banker's real product is the counterfactual: every bidder must believe someone else will pay more.

The 2-round auction, step by step
  1. Preparation (4 to 8 weeks). CIM, management presentation, financial model with a defensible adjusted-EBITDA bridge, sell-side QoE, data room. The buyer list: strategics by strategic fit, sponsors by platform logic and fund capacity.
  2. Round 1 (4 to 6 weeks). Teaser + NDA out to 30 to 150 names; process letter sets the rules; indications of interest come back with value ranges. Cull to 4 to 8.
  3. Round 2 (4 to 6 weeks). Management meetings, full data room, draft SPA in the room. Final bids arrive with markups: price is compared net of the contract's risk allocation.
  4. Exclusivity and signing (1 to 3 weeks). Confirmatory diligence, financing papers, disclosure schedules.
The math that decides it
Bids are compared on TEV, then translated to equity value through the debt-like items schedule: unfunded pensions, earnouts owed, capitalized leases, working-capital peg deltas. A $500M headline bid with a $20M working-capital fight and $15M of debt-like surprises loses to a clean $480M. The peg negotiation is where 1 to 3% of deal value quietly moves.
What goes wrong
  • Leaks reprice the deal or kill it: customer and employee flight starts the day the story runs.
  • The broad-auction trap: 100 NDAs signal desperation; the best buyers step back and wait for the process to fail.
  • Re-trading: a bidder wins with a number, then chips it in exclusivity. Antidote: keep the runner-up warm.
Buy-side & take-privates the other chair

Buy-side advisory sells judgment, not process: what the target is worth to this buyer, how to win without overpaying, and how to get the board's yes. Take-privates add a second game: public-company law.

The take-private sequence
  1. The approach. A private bear hug to the board (public if the board stonewalls). The board forms a special committee if insiders or a controlling holder are involved.
  2. Price discovery. The committee's bankers run a market check or negotiate a go-shop: 25 to 45 days post-signing to solicit topping bids, with a reduced break fee for a go-shop jumper.
  3. Financing certainty. Committed debt papers and an equity commitment letter; reverse termination fee (4 to 7% of equity) prices the buyer's walk-away right.
  4. The vote. Proxy out, ISS/Glass Lewis recommendations, majority (or majority-of-the-minority) approval, then closing on regulatory clearance.
The math
Ability-to-pay LBO: solve for the maximum price at which the sponsor still clears its hurdle. Max TEV = equity check x target MOIC solved backward through exit multiple, leverage, and FCF paydown. If public holders expect a 30% premium and the ability-to-pay lands at 18%, there is no deal, and saying so early is the advice.
What goes wrong
  • Appraisal and fiduciary suits when process looks pre-wired: the committee's independence is the deal's armor.
  • Financing outs in volatile markets: the 2022 vintage of busted take-privates was a lesson in reverse-termination-fee design.
  • Winner's curse: the buy-side banker who never says "walk" is a fee machine, not an advisor.
Activism defense & hostile situations the war room

When the buyer or the shareholder will not take no. Defense work is retained before the fight: the vulnerability assessment, the break-glass plan, the relationships with the proxy advisors.

The playbook, both directions
  • Bear hug: a public offer letter designed to put the board in play. Response calculus: reject, engage, or run a process on your own terms.
  • Tender offer: straight to shareholders; the poison pill (shareholder rights plan) caps accumulation, usually at 10 to 15%, and forces negotiation.
  • Proxy fight: universal proxy cards made single-seat campaigns cheap; the math is registered votes, not arguments. ISS and Glass Lewis recommendations swing 20 to 30% of the vote.
  • The activist screen: underperformance vs peers, conglomerate discount, lazy balance sheet, governance hooks. Defense means fixing the screen before someone else monetizes it.
The players

Activists: Elliott, Starboard, Icahn, Third Point, Trian. Defense: Goldman, Morgan Stanley, Evercore, Moelis, plus Wachtell on the legal side and the proxy solicitors (Innisfree, Okapi) counting votes nightly.

What goes wrong
  • Fighting the last war: a pill deters accumulation, not a good argument. Settling for board seats early is often cheaper than a proxy loss.
  • The clock: advance-notice windows decide whether a campaign can even launch this year; missing them is malpractice on either side.
Fairness opinions & the merger model the technical core

The fairness opinion is the board's liability shield and the desk's most standardized product: an opinion that the consideration is fair, from a financial point of view, supported by the full valuation stack.

What is actually in the book
  1. Trading comps and precedent transactions (with control premiums), footnoted to death.
  2. DCF with sensitivity tables on WACC and terminal growth or exit multiple.
  3. Accretion/dilution: pro forma EPS under cash, stock, and mixed consideration; the breakeven synergy number.
  4. Contribution analysis and exchange-ratio analysis in stock deals: who brings what % of EBITDA vs who gets what % of the combined equity.
  5. Premiums paid: 1-day, 30-day, 52-week-high reference points.
The math worth memorizing
All-stock deal breakeven: accretive when the acquirer's P/E exceeds the target's deal P/E (after synergies). Cash deal: accretive when the target's after-tax earnings yield beats the after-tax cost of the cash (foregone interest or new debt). Breakeven synergies = (deal P/E x target NI shortfall) solved to zero EPS impact. Every interview question in this family is one of these 2 identities rearranged.
What goes wrong
  • Synergy inflation: "fully loaded" synergies that ignore dis-synergies (lost customers, retention comp) make bad deals look accretive.
  • The single-opinion trap: conflicted advisors (financing the buyer while opining for the seller) invite a second opinion and litigation discovery.
Antitrust & cross-border the closing risk

Signing is the middle of the deal. The regulatory desk prices the gap between signing and closing: who bears the risk that a government says no, and what the buyer must give to get to yes.

The gauntlet
  • HSR (US): filing, 30-day wait, then the second request that turns months into a year. The 2023 merger guidelines lowered the structural thresholds regulators sue on.
  • EC (Europe): Phase I (25 working days) or Phase II (90+); remedies negotiated as commitments.
  • CFIUS: national-security review of foreign acquirers; can force divestiture years later if skipped.
  • China SAMR: the long pole in global tech and semiconductor deals; conditional approvals with behavioral remedies.
How the contract prices it
Efforts covenants ("hell or high water" vs "reasonable best efforts"), divestiture caps (buyer must sell up to $X of assets to clear), reverse termination fees for antitrust failure, and outside dates with extensions. A tight covenant package is worth real dollars: the seller's lawyers trade price for certainty on every one of these lines.
What goes wrong
  • Remedy underestimation: the fix-it-first divestiture that satisfies no one, or a divestiture buyer regulators reject.
  • Interloper risk during a long review: an 18-month clearance window is an invitation to topping bids and macro turns.
