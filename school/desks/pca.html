5 The sub-desks: open each one

Five businesses share the PCA umbrella. Each panel below is a self-contained module: the trade, the process, the math, the players, and what goes wrong.

LP-led secondaries the classic trade

An LP sells its existing fund interests: the buyer pays cash today for the remaining NAV plus the obligation to fund the unfunded commitments. The seller gets liquidity and balance-sheet room; the buyer gets seasoned, partly de-risked exposure at a discount, with a shorter duration than a blind pool.

The process
  1. Portfolio triage. The advisor splits the book: funds that will clear near par, funds that need a story, tail-end positions that may go to a mosaic (split) sale.
  2. Data room. Quarterly reports, schedules of investments, capital-account statements. The buyer rebuilds the portfolio company by company for the 10 largest positions and models the rest top-down on a pacing curve.
  3. Bids as % of NAV off a reference date (usually the latest quarter-end). Distributions after the reference date reduce the purchase price dollar-for-dollar; capital calls add to it. The economics belong to the buyer from the reference date.
  4. Exclusivity, then consents. Every underlying GP must consent to the transfer, and many hold rights of first refusal. Consent chasing is where timelines die.
  5. Close with a purchase-price adjustment mechanism, often in 2 tranches for very large books.
The math
Buy a buyout book at 92% of a $100M NAV. Underwrite gross cash flows of 1.55x paid over a 5-year pacing curve: that solves to roughly a mid-teens gross IRR. Rules of thumb: every 5 points of discount is worth about 1 to 1.5 points of IRR; venture books price 10 to 20 points below buyout because the marks are older and the pacing longer.
The players

Buyers: Ardian, Lexington (Franklin Templeton), Blackstone Strategic Partners, HarbourVest, AlpInvest, Goldman Vintage. Advisors: PJT Park Hill, Evercore PCA, Jefferies, Campbell Lutyens, Lazard.

What goes wrong
  • Stale marks. Paying 95% of a NAV that is about to be written down 15% is paying 112%. The 2022 denominator-effect wave cleared cheap partly because buyers did not trust the marks.
  • Unfunded surprise. A 90% headline price on a fund that is 60% unfunded is mostly a forward commitment, not a discount.
  • ROFR games. GPs can hand your price to a preferred buyer. Advisors sequence consents to manage this.
GP-led secondaries & continuation vehicles the growth engine

The GP moves 1 asset (or several) out of an aging fund into a new vehicle the same GP manages. Existing LPs choose: sell at the deal price or roll into the CV. A lead secondary buyer underwrites the price, anchors the new capital, and negotiates the terms. This is now roughly half of all secondaries volume, and single-asset CVs are its sharpest edge: a full LBO underwrite of one company, plus a governance negotiation.

The flavors
  • Single-asset CV. The trophy the GP will not sell. Buyout-grade diligence on one name.
  • Multi-asset CV. 2 to 6 assets; diversification lowers the underwriting bar and usually the return target.
  • Strip sale. The fund sells a fractional strip of several assets but keeps control; a CV without the full move.
  • Tender + staple. Buyer tenders for LP interests and commits primary capital to the GP's next fund. The staple is the price of access, and the conflict everyone watches.
The process
  1. Advisor frames price and fairness; pre-marketing to 5 to 10 potential leads.
  2. The lead sets price, sizes its check, and negotiates economics: management fee near 1% on committed, carry often tiered (for example 10% over an 8% hurdle, 15% over 12%, 20% over 15%).
  3. Syndication fills the book below the lead.
  4. LP election window (ILPA asks for 20 business days and a true status-quo option). LPAC waives the conflict.
  5. Close: selling LPs cash out, rolling LPs continue, the GP's crystallized carry rolls into the CV (100% rolled is the cleanest signal money can buy).
The math
Asset carried at $1.0B NAV. Lead underwrites a 2.0x in 5 years at par: that is about a 15% gross IRR before the new carry load. If the deal prices at a 5% discount and the GP rolls all carry, the rolling LP's question is symmetric: would you buy this company today at this price? If not, sell.
What goes wrong
  • Adverse selection, inverted. The classic objection ("if it is so good, why sell?") has a real answer: fund life, not asset quality. But when the answer is asset quality, the CV is where it hides.
  • Conflicted price discovery. The GP sits on both sides. The lead's independent underwrite, the fairness opinion, and the election option are the 3 checks; when all 3 are soft, LPs get hurt.
  • Default-to-roll. LPs without secondaries teams tend to take the status quo without underwriting it. ILPA's push for longer windows exists because of this.
Co-investment fee-free alongside

An LP writes equity directly into a sponsor's deal alongside the fund, typically with no management fee and no carry. The sponsor gets a bigger equity check without over-concentrating its own fund; the LP gets the sponsor's deal at the sponsor's basis, minus the 2-and-20.

Why the fee math dominates
A 2/20 fund turning 2.2x gross hands the LP roughly 1.8x net. The identical deal done as a fee-free co-invest nets the full 2.2x. Blend a program 50/50 between fund and co-invest and the effective fee load on the whole PE allocation drops by roughly a third, worth 100+ bps of net IRR at scale.
The process, at sprint speed
  1. Sponsor circulates the opportunity, usually 2 to 4 weeks before signing or between signing and close.
  2. The co-investor reads the sponsor's own IC memo and model, commissions nothing new that takes weeks, focuses on 2 or 3 kill questions (market, customer concentration, the exit multiple assumption).
  3. Legal: a co-invest vehicle LPA, allocation and exit alignment terms (tag rights, no worse than the fund).
  4. Fund and close alongside; report quarterly at the sponsor's marks.
The skill

Selection and speed. The deals shown most freely are the ones sponsors cannot fill, so a disciplined program tracks its sponsors' hit rates and says no fast. The buy side here: HarbourVest, Hamilton Lane, StepStone, AlpInvest, Neuberger, plus the large pensions (CPP, CalPERS) running direct programs.

What goes wrong
  • Bandwidth. A 400-page data room and 10 business days. Programs without dedicated staff become adverse-selection magnets.
  • Concentration creep. Co-invests cluster in the vintages and sectors where sponsors are busiest, exactly when prices are highest.
  • No steering wheel. If the deal sours, the co-investor holds a minority sliver with no governance rights and the sponsor's timeline.
GP stakes own the manager

Buy 10 to 25% of the management company itself: a claim on fee streams, carry, and balance-sheet investments across every current and future fund. Permanent capital, no exit required by the structure, and the seller is usually funding its own growth: GP commitments to the next flagship, founder liquidity, succession.

The valuation build
Value = FRE x multiple + PV(carry) + balance sheet. A manager producing $150M of fee-related earnings on a growing platform might carry a 20x FRE multiple ($3.0B), plus carry by vintage discounted at 15 to 25% (say $600M), plus GP commitments at NAV ($400M): $4.0B total, so a 20% stake costs $800M. The swing factor is almost always the next flagship: fundraising trajectory moves FRE more than margins do.
Diligence that matters
  • Fundraising trajectory: next flagship vs last, re-up rates, LP concentration.
  • FRE margin: 50 to 60% is best-in-class; below 40% says the platform is buying growth.
  • Key-person depth: a stake in a firm is a stake in 5 people unless proven otherwise.
  • Carry quality: vintage spread, European vs American waterfalls, clawback exposure.
The players

Blue Owl (Dyal), Blackstone GP Stakes, Petershill (Goldman), Bonaccord, Hunter Point, Armen. The bank PCA desks run the sale processes.

The open question

Exits. The industry has bought hundreds of stakes and sold few. The candidate routes: IPO of the manager, GP buybacks, strip sales to newer stakes funds, and securitizations of the fee streams. Until the routes are proven, GP-stakes funds are priced partly on yield (the fee stream pays a coupon-like distribution) rather than on realized multiples.

Fund placement & primaries the origination floor

Raising the funds in the first place. The placement agent's product is its LP network: who allocates to what strategy, at what size, on what calendar. Everything else on this desk grew out of that network: the LPs you place funds with are the LPs who later need liquidity (LP-led flow) and the GPs you raise for are the GPs who later run CVs (GP-led flow).

The process
  1. Mandate: the agent qualifies the GP (track record attribution, team stability, strategy drift) before taking it to market. A failed raise burns the agent's LP relationships, so agents curate hard.
  2. Materials: PPM, DDQ, quartile-positioned track record, case studies.
  3. Premarketing: soft-circling anchors; first-close incentives (fee discounts for early money).
  4. Closings over 12 to 18 months; the final close is a negotiation about who gets cut back, in hot funds, and who gets chased, in cold ones.
The economics

Placement fees run around 2% of capital raised from introduced LPs, sometimes with trails. In a cold fundraising market the primaries calendar stalls and the same LP conversations convert into secondaries mandates: exit-starved GPs run CVs, over-allocated LPs sell books. The desk hedges itself.

The players

PJT Park Hill, Evercore Private Funds, Campbell Lutyens, Lazard Private Capital Advisory, Jefferies, Moelis Private Funds Advisory, UBS PFG.
