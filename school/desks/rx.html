6 The sub-desks: open each one

Restructuring is one desk with 5 distinct games running at once. Open each panel for the sequence, the math, and how each side loses.

Chapter 11, end to end the main event

A federal court process for reorganizing a business while it keeps operating. The automatic stay freezes every creditor at the door; the fight moves from collection to valuation and priority.

The case, in sequence
  1. First-day motions: pay employees and critical vendors, keep cash management running, approve interim DIP. The judge's first read of the case's competence.
  2. DIP financing: super-priority, often priming, loaded with milestones (file a plan by X, sale by Y). Whoever writes the DIP writes the calendar.
  3. Exclusivity (120 days, extendable to 18 months): only the debtor may propose a plan; the leverage every debtor advisor guards.
  4. Plan and disclosure statement: classes of claims, treatment of each, the valuation that decides who gets what.
  5. Confirmation: each impaired class votes (two-thirds in amount, half in number). A dissenting class can be crammed down under 1129(b) if the plan is fair and equitable and respects absolute priority.
The math
Everything reduces to the waterfall drawn above: distributable value less admin and priority claims, paid down the priority stack until it runs out. The class where it runs out, the fulcrum, converts to equity and owns the company. Move the enterprise value $200M and the fulcrum moves a class: that is why the valuation fight is the case.
What goes wrong
  • Administrative burn: $10 to 30M a month of professionals in a big case; slow cases consume the estate they argue over.
  • The free-fall filing: no RSA, no DIP arranged, trade flees; prepacks exist because free-falls destroy value.
Out-of-court & prepacks the cheaper path

Most restructurings never see a courtroom. The debtor and its largest creditors negotiate privately, sign a restructuring support agreement (RSA), and either close out of court or use a short prepackaged filing to bind holdouts.

The spectrum
  • Pure out-of-court: exchange offers plus consents; works only when participation clears 90%+ because holdouts keep their old claims.
  • Prepack: votes solicited before filing; in and out of Chapter 11 in 1 to 45 days. Binds 100% once confirmed.
  • Pre-negotiated: RSA signed with anchor creditors, votes solicited in court; 2 to 4 months.
The RSA, the desk's real product

Who supports the deal, what they get (exchange terms, fees, backstop economics), milestones, and a fiduciary out. Backstop parties commit to fund new money and are paid 5 to 10% in fees and discounted allocations for it: the economics that make ad hoc groups form early and fight hard.

The math
Holdout arithmetic runs the structure choice: an exchange at 80 that gets 95% participation still leaves 5% of old bonds due at par. If that stub is small enough to pay, stay out of court; if it can accelerate or sue, the prepack binds it. Cost comparison: out-of-court ~$20 to 50M of fees vs $150M+ for a contested Chapter 11.
What goes wrong
  • Participation misses the threshold and the deal converts to a filing anyway, months later and weaker.
  • Coercive-exchange litigation: exit consents that strip covenants from non-participants invite years of suits.
Creditor-side representation the other war room

Every debtor mandate creates 3 or 4 creditor mandates: the ad hoc first-lien group, the crossover group, the unsecured committee, sometimes an equity committee. Creditor work is portfolio triage plus game theory, billed monthly.

How groups form and fight
  1. The organization meeting: holders compare positions, sign co-op agreements (no one exits or trades against the group), hire counsel and a banker.
  2. Diligence under NDA: the group goes private on information the market lacks; restricted lists and information walls follow.
  3. The ask: a counter-waterfall, a DIP proposal of their own, or a plan term sheet; leverage comes from blocking positions (one-third of a class stops a cramdown vote).
  4. The settlement: most fights end in an RSA where the loudest group backstops the new money and books the fees.
The math
A blocking stake in the fulcrum class is the cheapest control in finance: one-third of a $600M class bought at 45 is a $90M outlay that can veto any plan touching that class, and usually converts to board seats and backstop economics. Co-op agreements exist because uptiers (Serta) taught minorities what majorities can do to them.
What goes wrong
  • Group fracture: a debtor's exchange offer engineered to peel off half the group; the co-op agreement is the countermeasure and the courts are still pricing its limits.
  • Paying for yesterday's collateral: the drop-down already moved the good assets; the liens the group holds secure a shell.
363 sales & distressed M&A the auction inside the case

Section 363 lets a debtor sell assets free and clear of liens, faster and cleaner than any plan. For buyers it is the best M&A market that exists: motivated seller, court-blessed title, no reps surviving close.

The choreography
  1. Stalking horse: a first bidder sets the floor in exchange for a 2 to 4% break fee, expense reimbursement, and bid protections (minimum overbid increments).
  2. Bid procedures approved, marketing period runs 30 to 60 days: the banker's classic sell-side sprint under a judge's clock.
  3. The auction: open outcry against the stalking horse; secured creditors may credit bid their debt up to its face amount.
  4. Sale hearing: the court approves the highest and best bid; closing follows in days.
The math
Credit-bid arithmetic: a lender owed $400M on assets marketed at $250M can bid its paper instead of cash, effectively buying the company at its debt's market price (bought at 60, credit-bid at par). Cash bidders must beat the face amount of the debt, not its trading price, which is why loan-to-own funds buy the paper first and the auction second.
What goes wrong
  • The chilled auction: a too-rich break fee or a too-fast timeline scares real bidders off; judges have learned to trim both.
  • Melting-ice-cube pricing: genuine urgency (Joann, Spirit's retail vintage) means every week of process is margin walking out the door.
The valuation fight where cases are won

Recoveries are a function of enterprise value, so every side hires a banker to prove the number that pays its class. The junior classes argue the business is worth more (value reaches them); seniors argue it is worth less (they own it all). Same company, same data room, $700M apart, under oath.

How the fight is fought
  • Dueling expert reports: DCF, comps, precedents, each assumption chosen from the defensible end of its range in the client's direction.
  • The tells: management projections (whose case were they built for?), the DIP budget's own implied multiple, actual market bids from any 363 process, trading prices of the debt itself.
  • Cross-examination: the other side's banker walks the court through your football field one cherry-picked comp at a time.
The math
A senior-friendly case: 5.5x on trough EBITDA of $180M = $990M, value breaks in the first lien, juniors get warrants. The junior case: 7.0x on normalized $230M = $1.6B, first lien covered at par, juniors own the equity. The judge picks a point (or the parties settle at the courthouse steps), and every professional fee in the case was spent arguing that interval.
What goes wrong
  • Winning the trial, losing the exit: a confirmed valuation the market refuses to pay at emergence (the new equity trades at half the court's number).
  • Model theater: a 10-year DCF on a business that missed 8 straight quarters persuades no one; credibility, not precision, wins valuation trials.
