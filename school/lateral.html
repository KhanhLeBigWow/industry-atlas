The lateral guide.

Moving bank to bank is a different game from campus recruiting: faster processes, deal-heavy interviews, and an unforgiving premium on your story. This is how lateral hiring actually works, and exactly what to study for the seat you want, group by group.

L.1 How lateral processes actually run

Campus recruiting is a scheduled harvest; lateral hiring is opportunistic and continuous. Seats open when someone leaves, a group wins more mandates than it can staff, or a new team lifts out. That changes everything about timing and preparation.

The 4 channels, in rough order of volume: recruiters/headhunters who cover banking seats (they call analysts around the 1-year mark; answer politely and stay warm even when uninterested), direct applications to posted roles (boutiques post real openings on their own sites and LinkedIn, and the posting text tells you the interview), referrals from former colleagues and school networks (the highest-conversion channel: a warm intro converts a resume drop into a first round), and being lifted with a senior banker who moves platforms.

Speed is the defining feature. A lateral process can run from first call to offer in 2 to 3 weeks: a screen with HR or the COO, 1 or 2 technical rounds with associates and VPs, then a partner round that is mostly judgment and fit. There is rarely a second chance to "get technical later": you interview at the level of the seat from the first call, so the preparation happens before the process starts, not during it.

Logistics that trip people: keep the search confidential (interview in the morning before work, never from the office); expect references from people who have seen your work, timed late in the process; know your bonus cycle (moving before payout is negotiable: buyouts exist, but only if you raise it); and resign gracefully with 2 weeks and zero drama, because this industry has a memory.

L.2 The lateral story: why leave, why here

The single most-failed lateral question is not technical. It is the story. Interviewers are pricing 2 risks: that you are running FROM something (and will bring it with you), and that you will leave THEM as easily.

The formula: run-toward, not run-from. Anchor the move on what the target seat uniquely offers: deal flow in a product you want reps in, more senior exposure per deal, a franchise in the thing you want to master (restructuring, sponsors, a sector). Then make your current seat part of the logic: "my group taught me X, and the natural place to compound it is Y." One sentence of honest, non-bitter context about the current seat is fine ("our deal flow in the product I care about has thinned"); a paragraph of grievance is disqualifying.

The consistency test. Your story must survive: why banking (again), why this product, why this firm, why now, and: would you stay if your current group promoted you tomorrow? Rehearse those 5 as a set; the partner round will probe for daylight between them.

L.3 Deal walkthroughs: the lateral centerpiece

Campus candidates get textbook questions; laterals get their own resume. Expect 20 of 30 technical minutes on YOUR transactions, at whatever depth you claim. The bar: you can defend every number on the page.

Prepare 2 or 3 deals in this structure: context (client, situation, why the deal existed), your role (the specific workstreams YOU ran: the model, the marketing materials, diligence coordination: never "we" for work you did or "I" for work you did not), the analysis (headline numbers cold: size, multiple, premium, leverage, the key sensitivity that mattered), the outcome, and 1 judgment layer: what you would have done differently, what surprised you, what the client debated. That last layer is what separates a lateral from a first-year.

Confidentiality discipline: for unannounced deals, genericize (sector, size band, structure) and say you are doing so: interviewers respect it and notice its absence. Everything announced is fair game at full detail.

The technical bar by level. Analyst-to-analyst moves still get accounting and valuation fundamentals (the interview bank covers the canon). Analyst-to-associate and associate moves get fewer textbook questions and harder applied ones: walk through the model YOU built, defend a multiple YOU chose, react to a live fact pattern in the group's product. Refresh fundamentals for a week; rehearse your deals for 3.

L.5 Worked example: a restructuring new-business seat

Take a real posting archetype: an analyst role in a top restructuring group (PJT's RSSG, Houlihan, Evercore, Lazard, Moelis run versions of it) focused on new business research: screening for the next mandate. The job description IS the syllabus. Read it like one.

"Screen and identify companies/industries that are at risk of increased defaults... financial modeling work to analyze company cash flows... knowledge of the high-yield/distressed/leveraged loan market... ability to build basic cash flow financial models."

Decoded into a study plan:

  • Distressed screening: build and defend a screen: leverage and coverage trajectories, maturity walls inside 24 months, loans under 90 / bonds yielding 12%+, revolver draws, covenant headroom. Practice by naming 3 sectors under stress right now and 2 candidate companies in each, with the numbers.
  • The leveraged credit market: know the map: high-yield vs leveraged loans vs private credit (sizes, buyers, covenant regimes), where spreads are today, what the default rate is doing, and this cycle's themes (liability management, maturity walls, private-credit rescue capital).
  • Cash flow modeling: be able to build a 13-week cash flow and a simple 3-statement burn model from scratch: receipts, disbursements, revolver mechanics, the week liquidity dies. The Toolkit and Module 03 rebuild the mechanics.
  • Chapter 11 literacy: the automatic stay, DIP financing, 363 sales, plan confirmation and cram-down, the fulcrum. Follow 2 live cases end to end: read the first-day declarations (free on the case dockets) and the DIP terms.
  • The group's own record: know their marquee mandates (for RSSG: names like PG&E, WeWork, Revlon, Puerto Rico appear in their own materials) and be ready to discuss 1 intelligently: who advised whom, what the structure was, what was contested.
  • Sector stress maps: for 2 or 3 industries, know the distress transmission: what breaks first when rates rise or demand falls. Every full profile in the Industry Atlas carries a risk register built for exactly this question.
  • AI-tool fluency: postings now ask for it explicitly. Have a concrete answer: how you use AI tools to accelerate screening, summarize dockets and filings, and draft materials: with the judgment layer (verification, confidentiality) that shows you use them like a professional.

Presentation matters as much as analysis in new-business seats: the output of the role is partner-ready pages. Tight formatting, sourced numbers, and a one-line so-what per page.

L.6 The 30-day lateral prep plan

  • Week 1: rewrite the resume deal-first (each deal: size, your workstreams, 1 outcome); refresh accounting and EV/valuation fundamentals from the interview bank.
  • Week 2: build the 2 or 3 deal walkthroughs; rehearse aloud until the numbers are automatic; draft the 5-question story set (why leave, why here, why product, why now, would you stay).
  • Week 3: group-specific depth (the table above); for RX seats, the worked example's 7 items; re-read the target firm's last 12 months of announced mandates.
  • Week 4: live rounds simulation: markets levels each morning, 1 mock technical with a friend, references warmed, story stress-tested by someone skeptical.
