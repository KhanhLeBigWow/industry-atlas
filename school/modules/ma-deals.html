Module 05 · Advanced

M&A & the Deal Process

How companies are bought and sold, from the first pitch to the closing dinner. M&A is where valuation stops being theory: a real buyer writes a real check, and every number in this module decides how big it is.

05.1 Why deals happen (and why many should not)

Every acquisition is a bet that two companies are worth more together than apart. The strategic logic usually sounds airtight in the board deck. The empirical record says the hard part is not finding the logic, it is refusing to overpay for it. Understanding both halves, the rationale and the record, is what separates a deal advisor from a deal cheerleader.

Start with the buyer's side of the argument. Nearly every deal rationale you will ever hear is a variation on 4 themes:

Scale

Bigger spreads fixed costs over more revenue. One headquarters instead of 2, one salesforce covering both product lines, better pricing from suppliers. Scale deals live or die on cost synergies, which is why they are the easiest to underwrite.

Capabilities

Buy versus build. A technology, a license, a customer base, or a team that would take 5 years to replicate can be bought in 5 months. Pharma buying biotech pipelines and industrials buying software businesses are both capability deals.

Consolidation

In a fragmented industry, rolling up competitors takes out duplicate cost and, quietly, takes out pricing pressure. Serial acquirers in waste, dental practices, and HVAC services run this playbook dozens of times over.

Defense

Sometimes you buy so a rival cannot. Blocking a competitor from acquiring a key supplier, or acquiring the disruptor before it gets big enough to hurt you. Defensive deals are the hardest to value because the benefit is a loss avoided, not a cash flow gained.

Notice that all 4 arguments reduce to the same claim: 1 + 1 > 2. The combined company will produce more cash than the 2 standalone companies did. The entire deal negotiation is really an argument over who pockets the extra: the buyer, or the seller who demands to be paid for it upfront.

The most persistent finding in M&A research is that the average deal creates value overall but the acquirer captures little or none of it. Across decades of event studies, target shareholders gain roughly 20% to 30% on announcement while acquirer returns cluster around zero, and a large fraction of acquirers see their stock fall. The mechanism is the winner's curse: in a competitive auction the winning bid tends to come from the most optimistic buyer, and optimism is not a synergy. Announced synergies are a forecast; the premium is a wire transfer.

Who buys: strategics vs financial sponsors

Every sell-side banker sorts the universe of buyers into 2 species, because they bid differently, move differently, and break differently.

Strategic buyerFinancial sponsor
Who they areAn operating company in the same or an adjacent industryA private equity fund investing committed capital from its limited partners
Why they buyScale, capabilities, consolidation, defenseReturns: buy, improve, and sell in roughly 4 to 6 years
How they payCash on hand, their own stock, balance sheet debtFund equity plus substantial new debt raised against the target itself
SynergiesReal cost overlaps they can underwrite into the priceUsually none, unless bolting onto an existing portfolio company (an add-on)
Ceiling on priceHighest when overlap is large; can rationally outbid everyoneCapped by leverage limits and the fund's return target

A well-run process invites both. Strategics set the ceiling on value because synergies let them pay more and still come out ahead. Sponsors set the floor on certainty: they move fast, they do not leak your customer list to a competitor, and they close. The tension between the highest price and the safest path to close runs through everything that follows.

05.2 The cast: who does what on a deal

A live deal is a temporary company staffed by advisors, each with a distinct job and a distinct fee model. Knowing who is in the room, who hired them, and how they get paid tells you most of what you need to predict their behavior.

AdvisorHired byWhat they actually do
Sell-side M&A bankerThe seller's boardRuns the auction: positioning, materials, buyer outreach, bid choreography, negotiation. Paid a success fee at closing, often 1% to 2% of deal value on large deals and higher percentages below roughly $100M.
Buy-side M&A bankerThe acquirerValuation, bid tactics, financing structure, board materials. Fee is usually smaller than the sell-side fee and sometimes partly fixed.
M&A lawyersEach side separatelyDraft and negotiate the purchase agreement, run regulatory strategy, manage closing mechanics. Billed hourly, deal or no deal.
Accountants (QoE)Mostly buyers; sellers increasingly pre-empt with their ownQuality of earnings (QoE) work: verifying that the EBITDA the price is based on is real, recurring, and correctly calculated.
ConsultantsEither sideCommercial due diligence: a market study testing whether the growth story in the marketing materials survives contact with customers.
Financing banksThe buyerCommit the debt that funds the purchase. Their financing fees on a large leveraged deal can exceed the M&A advisory fee.

Who the banker really works for

Formally, the sell-side banker signs an engagement letter with the company and answers to its board. Economically, the success fee aligns the banker with a high price: 1.25% of $1.2B beats 1.25% of $1.0B. But the sharper incentive is binary. The fee is typically all-or-nothing at closing, so the banker's strongest pull is toward a deal happening, not necessarily the perfect deal. Good boards understand this and treat banker advice on "take the bird in hand" moments with appropriate skepticism.

There is a second, softer incentive worth knowing: league tables. Banks are ranked publicly by deal volume, and rankings drive future mandates. A banker will sometimes fight for a marginal deal because the franchise needs the credit, not because the fee is large.

Fairness opinions

Before a public company board approves a sale, it almost always obtains a fairness opinion: a short letter from an investment bank stating that the consideration is fair, from a financial point of view, to shareholders. The opinion is backed by a valuation analysis (DCF, comparable companies, precedent transactions, the same toolkit from Module 04) presented to the board.

The polite critique of fairness opinions: they are purchased by the party that wants the deal approved, sometimes from the same bank earning a success fee if it closes, and the range of "fair" is generous. Courts and boards still value them because they document that directors followed a careful process, which is the real legal shield. Read a fairness opinion as evidence of process, not as an independent verdict on price.

05.3 The sell-side process, end to end

The auction is the sell-side banker's core product: a manufactured market for a company that trades exactly once. Everything about it, the staged information release, the deadlines, the parallel bidders, exists to create competitive tension and keep leverage with the seller for as long as possible.

Processes come in flavors. A broad auction contacts 75 to 150 buyers and maximizes competition at the cost of confidentiality. A targeted process approaches 10 to 25 carefully chosen names, trading some price tension for speed and discretion. A negotiated sale deals with a single buyer, usually because they pre-empted with a number too good to shop. The timeline below is a classic mid-market auction; add or subtract weeks for size, complexity, and regulatory heat. Call the seller Juniper Analytics, code name Project Juniper.

  1. Weeks 1-4
    Preparation and materials

    The engagement letter is signed and the working group forms. The banker builds the model (a projection model that frames the valuation story), drafts the teaser (a 1-2 page anonymous summary with financial highlights but no company name), and writes the CIM, the confidential information memorandum: a 50-100+ page book covering the business, market, financials, and growth plan. In parallel the team builds a tiered buyer list of strategics and sponsors and stands up the skeleton of the dataroom.

  2. Weeks 5-6
    Launch and outreach

    The teaser goes out, broad or targeted per the strategy above. Interested buyers negotiate an NDA (non-disclosure agreement, often with non-solicit terms protecting employees) and receive the CIM plus a process letter setting the first-round bid deadline and required contents.

  3. Weeks 7-9
    First-round bids: IOIs

    Buyers submit IOIs, indications of interest: non-binding letters stating a valuation range, funding sources, key assumptions, and diligence needs. The banker's job is reading through the numbers: a sponsor at "$450M to $500M subject to financing" and a strategic at "$470M, no financing condition" are not the same bid. The board culls the field to a second round.

  4. Weeks 9-14
    Management presentations and dataroom

    The 3 to 8 remaining buyers each get a management presentation, a half-day session where the CEO and CFO pitch the business and take questions, plus access to the virtual dataroom: contracts, detailed financials, customer data, HR files. A managed Q&A process and site visits run alongside. This is the most operationally exhausting phase for the company, which still has to hit its numbers while being inspected.

  5. Weeks 14-16
    Final bids and contract markups

    A second process letter demands final bids: a firm price, evidence of committed financing, and a markup of the seller's draft purchase agreement showing exactly which terms the buyer accepts and which it rejects. Markups are as informative as price. A high number with an aggressive markup (heavy conditions, broad walk-away rights) can be worth less than a lower, cleaner bid. The banker levels bids on value, terms, and certainty together.

  6. Weeks 16-17
    Exclusivity and the LOI

    The winner signs an LOI, a letter of intent: mostly non-binding on price and structure, but binding on exclusivity, the seller's promise to stop talking to other buyers for a set window. The moment exclusivity is granted, leverage flips to the buyer, which is why good bankers keep the window short (30 to 45 days) and keep a runner-up warm.

  7. Weeks 17-21
    Confirmatory diligence

    The buyer's full diligence machine engages: quality of earnings, legal document review, tax, environmental, insurance, IT, background checks. Findings become negotiating chips. A $3M inventory issue rarely kills a deal; it reappears as a price reduction or an indemnity in the next phase.

  8. Weeks 19-22
    Negotiating and signing the SPA

    Lawyers converge on the SPA, the sale and purchase agreement (in a public merger, the merger agreement): price mechanics (locked box vs completion accounts, the working capital peg), representations and warranties, indemnities, covenants, and termination rights. Signing is announced publicly. The company is now sold on paper but not yet in fact.

  9. Weeks 22-30+
    Sign to close: regulatory and funding

    Between sign and close sit the conditions: antitrust clearance (in the US, the HSR filing under Hart-Scott-Rodino, with a waiting period that stretches to many months if regulators issue a second request), foreign filings, sometimes national security review (CFIUS), financing funding, third-party consents, and for public targets a shareholder vote. Big strategic deals can spend a year or more in this purgatory.

  10. Close
    Closing and funds flow

    A funds flow memo choreographs the wires: debt payoff, escrow funding, advisor fees, and the balance to sellers. Ownership transfers, the press release goes out, and the working group has the closing dinner. Post-closing purchase price adjustments and escrow releases play out over the following months.

The auction funnel

A typical broad process: many are teased, few are chosen. Illustrative counts for a mid-market deal.

Each stage exists to trade information for commitment: buyers get more data only after demonstrating more seriousness.

On leaks. 100 teasers means 100 chances for the process to leak, and leaks are expensive. Employees polish resumes, customers delay renewals, competitors whisper to your accounts that the ship is sinking, and any price talk that escapes becomes an anchor. Bankers mitigate with code names (Project Juniper, never the company name), tiered information release, clean teams for competitively sensitive data like customer-level pricing, and deliberately small first-round lists when the seller is fragile. If a process leaks to the press, the standard playbook is to say nothing and accelerate.

05.4 Deal math: premiums and synergies

Three numbers decide most deals: what the target is worth alone, the premium the buyer pays over that, and the synergies that are supposed to justify the premium. The arithmetic is simple. The discipline is not.

The premium

In a public deal, the offer is quoted as a premium over the unaffected price: the share price on the last trading day before deal news, leaks, or credible rumors began moving the stock. That last clause matters. If word slipped out 2 weeks ago and the stock is up 12%, measuring the premium off today's price understates what the buyer is really paying. Typical premiums for control of a public company run 20% to 40%, higher in competitive situations.

Premium = Offer price per share ÷ Unaffected price per share - 1

Why does control command a premium at all? Because the buyer is purchasing the right to change things: replace management, cut costs, redirect cash flows, sell assets. A minority share trades at the value of the business as it is run today; control is worth the value of the business as it could be run.

Synergies: the currency premiums are paid in

Cost synergies are expense reductions from combining: duplicate headquarters, overlapping salesforces, procurement scale, consolidated facilities. Revenue synergies are sales the combined firm supposedly wins that neither could alone: cross-selling each side's products to the other's customers, bundling, geographic extension.

Diligence teams credit these very differently, and for good reason. Cost synergies can be built bottom-up (this building, these 40 roles, this contract) and depend mostly on decisions inside management's control. Revenue synergies depend on customers choosing to buy more and competitors politely declining to respond. The working convention: underwrite most of the cost synergies, and treat revenue synergies as upside you do not pay for.

To value a synergy stream, convert it to after-tax cash flow and capitalize it like a perpetuity, then subtract the one-time cost of getting it (severance, systems integration, facility exits):

Synergy value = (Pre-tax run-rate synergies × (1 - Tax rate)) ÷ Discount rate - Costs to achieve

Harborline Foods buys Crestway Snacks. Crestway's unaffected equity value is $900M. Harborline offers a 30% premium, so it pays $1,170M, handing Crestway's shareholders $270M upfront. The integration plan identifies $40M of pre-tax run-rate cost synergies. At a 25% tax rate that is $30M of after-tax cash flow per year; capitalized at a 9% discount rate it is worth roughly $333M. One-time costs to achieve are $50M, so net synergy value is about $283M. Harborline paid $270M of premium to capture $283M of synergy value: the deal creates roughly $13M of value for the buyer if, and only if, every dollar of synergy shows up on schedule.

Where the synergy value goes

Harborline / Crestway worked example: the auction hands nearly all of the synergy value to the seller.

This is not a failure of the process. It is the process: a competitive auction is designed to make buyers bid their synergies away.

05.5 Accretion / dilution: the acquirer's first question

When a public company's board reviews a deal, the first slide they flip to asks one thing: does earnings per share go up or down? A deal that raises pro forma EPS is accretive; one that lowers it is dilutive. It is a crude test, and markets know it is crude, but it frames every public acquisition conversation.

The mechanics are bookkeeping. Pro forma net income is both companies' earnings, plus after-tax synergies, minus the after-tax cost of the money used to pay for the deal. Pro forma shares are the acquirer's shares plus whatever new stock it issues. Divide, and compare.

Accretion / (dilution) = Pro forma EPS ÷ Standalone EPS - 1

The intuition that makes the whole thing click: every funding source has a cost, expressed as an after-tax rate. New debt costs its interest rate times (1 - tax rate). Cash costs the yield you were earning on it, after tax. And stock costs your own earnings yield, which is 1 divided by your P/E: issuing shares at 20x earnings hands away 5% in EPS terms. The deal is accretive when the earnings you buy, measured as a yield on the price you pay, exceed the blended cost of that funding mix. Hence the classic all-stock shortcut: if your P/E is higher than the P/E you pay, you are paying with a cheaper currency than the earnings you receive, and EPS rises automatically.

Accretion is arithmetic, not value creation. A 25x company buying a 10x business with stock is always accretive on paper, even if the target deserves 10x because it is shrinking. The 1960s conglomerates industrialized this trick, boot-strapping EPS growth by serially acquiring cheaper earnings until the market caught on and the multiples collapsed. Use the tool below to build intuition, then remember that the DCF from Module 04, not the EPS math, is what decides whether a deal creates value.

05.6 Deal structures: who keeps which risk

Price gets the headline; structure decides who actually keeps the risk. Two deals at the same $1.2B can be wildly different economic propositions once you look at what is being bought, what it is being paid with, and what happens when something surfaces after closing.

Stock deal vs asset deal

In a stock deal the buyer purchases the legal entity itself: every asset, every contract, and every liability, including the ones nobody has discovered yet. In an asset deal the buyer picks assets and liabilities line by line, leaving the rest (and often the lawsuits) behind, and typically gets a stepped-up tax basis that generates depreciation shelter for years.

Stock dealAsset deal
What the buyer getsThe whole entity, everything it owns and owesSelected assets and liabilities, chosen line by line
Unknown liabilitiesTravel with the company to the buyerGenerally stay behind unless expressly assumed
Buyer taxCarryover basis, no step-up (absent special elections)Stepped-up basis, more depreciation and amortization shelter
Seller taxOne layer of tax, on the sharesCorporate sellers can face 2 layers, so they resist
Where you see itPublic mergers and most large dealsSmaller private deals, carve-outs, distressed sales

Cash vs stock: who bears market risk

Consideration allocates risk in time. Cash fixes the seller's value the moment the deal signs; from then on, every move in markets or in the combined business belongs to the buyer, and the seller pays tax now. Stock keeps the seller invested: they share the upside of the combination and the downside of the acquirer's stock.

Stock deals then split into 2 mechanics. With a fixed exchange ratio (say, 0.85 acquirer shares per target share) the number of shares is locked, so if the acquirer's stock falls 20% between sign and close, the target's shareholders eat it. With a fixed value structure the dollar amount is locked and the share count floats, pushing that risk back onto the acquirer's existing holders. Collars split the difference by bounding the ratio. Where the parties land tells you who had leverage and who believes in whose stock.

Bridging devices: earnouts, escrows, break fees

An earnout bridges a valuation gap with contingent payments: $80M now plus up to $20M more if revenue hits agreed targets over the next 2 years. Earnouts close deals that would otherwise die, and then generate more post-closing litigation than any other clause, because the buyer now controls the business whose performance triggers the payment.

Escrows and indemnities protect the buyer after closing in private deals. A slice of the price (commonly around 10%) sits in escrow for 12 to 24 months against breaches of the seller's representations. Increasingly, representations and warranties insurance replaces much of the escrow, letting sellers take home cleaner proceeds while the buyer claims against a policy instead.

Break fees price the divorce before the wedding. A target break fee (commonly 2% to 4% of equity value) compensates the buyer if the seller walks, typically to accept a superior offer. A reverse break fee runs the other way: the buyer pays if the deal dies for buyer-side reasons such as financing failure or antitrust rejection, and in regulatorily hot deals it can be much larger, because the seller is being asked to spend a year in limbo on the buyer's regulatory bet.

05.7 Defense and drama: when the target says no

Most deals are friendly, negotiated quietly and announced with matching quotes from both CEOs. The famous ones are not. When a board refuses to sell, a determined buyer can go around it, and an entire legal arsenal exists for exactly that fight.

A hostile bid is any attempt to buy a company over its board's objection. The gentlest opening is the bear hug: a public letter proposing a rich price, designed to force the board's hand by putting the offer in front of shareholders and the press. The blunter instrument is the tender offer: the bidder offers to buy shares directly from shareholders at a premium, bypassing the board entirely. If enough shares tender, the board's opinion stops mattering.

The defense with teeth is the poison pill (formally a shareholder rights plan). If any acquirer crosses a trigger threshold, often 10% to 15%, all other shareholders gain the right to buy new shares at a steep discount, massively diluting the raider's stake. No one has ever deliberately swallowed a modern pill; its purpose is to make hostile accumulation economically ruinous and force the bidder to negotiate with the board. Around it sit the supporting cast: staggered boards (only a third of directors up for election each year, so control takes 2 annual meetings to win), the white knight (a friendlier buyer invited in to top the hostile bid), and assorted exotica like the crown jewel sale and the Pac-Man defense (bid for the bidder), which is discussed more often than it is used.

Activist investors play a different game. They rarely want to buy the company; they buy 5% or so (crossing that threshold in the US requires a public 13D filing), then agitate: sell the company, break it up, return cash, replace directors. Activists are often the opening act of M&A, putting a company in play that management had no intention of selling.

Disney vs Comcast for 21st Century Fox, 2017-2019. In December 2017 Disney agreed to buy most of Fox's entertainment assets for roughly $52.4B in stock. In June 2018, a day after a court cleared the AT&T / Time Warner merger and de-risked the regulatory path, Comcast crashed the deal with an all-cash offer of about $65B. Disney came back within days at roughly $71.3B in cash and stock, and Fox's board took it. Comcast redirected its firepower to win the auction for Sky at about $39B, and Disney closed the Fox acquisition in March 2019. The saga is a compact lesson in everything this module covers: a motivated strategic underwriting synergies, a rival bidder adding roughly $19B to the price in 6 months, consideration switching between stock and cash as a competitive weapon, and regulatory strategy shaping the timing of every move.

The quiet lesson from the drama: hostile situations are rare, but the possibility of them disciplines every boardroom. A board that refuses a rich offer must be able to explain to its shareholders why staying independent is worth more. Sometimes it is. The burden of proof, though, sits with the people saying no.
