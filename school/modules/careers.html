Module 09 · Primer

Careers & Breaking In

The industry hires for a handful of very different seats, pays them very differently, and recruits for them absurdly early. Here is the honest map: the work, the money, the hours, and how people actually get in.

09.1 The role map

Nine seats cover most of the industry. They differ less in prestige than in what a Tuesday feels like: building models at midnight, making prices in milliseconds, or reading 10-Ks in blissful silence.

Investment banking

Advise companies on M&A and raise capital for them. Pitchbooks, models, deal processes. The classic entry door and the classic 80-hour week.

Love it if: you want deal reps and optionality. Hate it if: you value your weekends.

Sales & trading

Make markets and manage risk in real time. Intense from 6am to 5pm, then actually over. Seats are shrinking as machines take the simple flow.

Love it if: you want a scoreboard every day. Hate it if: you need slow, deep work.

Equity research

Publish investment views on a coverage list of public companies. Earnings season is brutal; the rest is reading, modeling, and talking to investors.

Love it if: you like being the expert. Hate it if: you want to own the decision.

Asset management

Invest other people's money in public markets over long horizons. Saner hours, slower feedback loops, careers measured in track records.

Love it if: you think in years. Hate it if: you need daily adrenaline.

Hedge funds

Absolute-return investing with leverage and shorting. Meritocratic and unforgiving: your P&L is your job security.

Love it if: you want maximum ownership of ideas. Hate it if: you need stability.

Private equity

Buy whole companies, improve them, sell them (Module 06). Deal work plus portfolio work; usually entered after 2 years of banking.

Love it if: you want to own businesses, not just advise them. Hate it if: you disliked banking hours (they only soften somewhat).

Venture capital

Fund startups, lose often, win rarely and enormously. Sourcing, judgment, and network matter more than modeling.

Love it if: you love founders and ambiguity. Hate it if: you need proof before deciding.

Corporate development

Run M&A from inside a company. Banking skills, human hours, one client (your CEO). Often the destination after banking or consulting.

Love it if: you want deals and a life. Hate it if: you will miss the deal volume.

Fintech & infrastructure

Build the rails: payments, exchanges, data, risk software. Finance knowledge compounds nicely with product and engineering skills.

Love it if: you want to build. Hate it if: you wanted the trading floor after all.

Choose by feedback loop, not by prestige. Trading tells you if you were right by lunch; private equity takes 5 years; venture takes 10. People burn out less from hours than from a feedback loop mismatched to their temperament.

09.3 A day in each life

Job titles hide more than they reveal. Hour by hour is where the truth lives.

9:30am Arrive, triage overnight comments from the VP on the CIM draft. 11am Turn model changes for a live sell-side; management call at noon. 2pm Comps update for a pitch nobody may ever read. 6pm Dinner at desk; the associate's "quick markup" lands at 7:45pm. 10pm Process the markup, ship to the printer. 12:30am Home, unless it is a live deal week, in which case it is not 12:30am. Reality: 70 to 85 hours in crunch, real weekends only when protected policies bite. What you get for it: 2 years of compressed reps that the buy-side pays a premium for.

6:15am At the desk reading overnight moves; morning meeting at 7. 9:30am Open. Quotes, hedges, client flow; lunch is something eaten with one hand. 2pm A client wants a price on size; 30 seconds of real risk decision. 4pm Close, P&L cut, risk report. 5:30pm Actually leave. The hours are humane; the intensity while present is not. Your P&L is public inside the firm, every day, forever.

7am Skim overnight news on the coverage list; a covered company pre-announced, morning note due by 8:15. 10am Model maintenance and a client call walking a PM through your thesis. 2pm Expert call on channel checks. 6pm Draft an initiation section. Normal weeks run 55 to 65 hours; earnings season runs on caffeine and adrenaline, 4 times a year. The craft: being the person the buy-side calls first.

9am Portfolio company board deck review. 11am New deal: turn the LBO model for the second-round bid, diligence calls with consultants at 2. 4pm Draft the investment committee memo section on customer concentration. 8pm Live deal, so dinner at desk; quiet weeks end at 7pm, signing weeks look like banking again. The shift from banking: fewer deliverables, more judgment, your name attached to the recommendation.

7:30am Read: filings, notes, the tape. 10am Rebuild the short thesis after last night's earnings beat went against you (down 40bps on the book). 1pm Management meeting with a company you may never own, because the option to know is the job. 5pm Pitch the PM one new idea; get 6 hard questions, keep 1 follow-up. Hours are moderate (55 to 65); the stress is that every position is a graded exam that never ends.

09.4 Recruiting timelines: earlier than you think

The industry's worst-kept dysfunction: seats are filled 1 to 2 years before the work starts. Missing a window does not end a career, but knowing the windows is a real edge.

  1. University year 1 to 2
    Foundation and first internships
    GPA protection, finance club, a first internship of any kind (boutique, wealth management, anything with a Bloomberg within eyeshot). Sophomore-year "insight programs" at large banks are quietly becoming first-round filters.
  2. Year 2, spring to summer
    IB summer analyst recruiting opens
    Applications for the junior-year summer internship open 12 to 14 months early. Networking calls, HireVue screens, superdays (back-to-back interview gauntlets). The summer internship converts to the full-time offer for most hires; full-time-only recruiting is a thin second door.
  3. Analyst year 1, autumn
    PE on-cycle strikes
    Mega-fund and upper-middle-market PE recruiting famously kicks off months into your first banking job, interviewing 22-year-olds for seats starting 2 years later. It starts with headhunter coffee chats and erupts into 48 hours of modeling tests and offers. Off-cycle processes run year-round for everyone else and are saner.
  4. Years 2 to 6
    Lateral and MBA routes
    Lateral hiring (bank to bank, or into corp dev, credit, HFs) runs continuously and rewards deal experience over pedigree. The MBA route re-opens banking at the associate level and remains the classic reset button for career changers.

The on-cycle trap: joining PE recruiting before you can comfortably build a paper LBO burns your one first impression with headhunters who gatekeep the industry. If you are not ready in October, skipping to off-cycle is a strategy, not a failure.

09.5 Breaking in, especially from a non-target

Banks recruit heavily from a short list of "target" schools, but every class contains people who came through the side door. The side door has a known shape.

The story. Every interview begins with "walk me through your resume," and the winning answer is a 90-second narrative arc: spark (why finance grabbed you), proof (what you did about it: internships, an investment club portfolio, a valuation you built for fun), destination (why this seat, this firm). Rehearse it until it sounds unrehearsed.

The networking math. Cold outreach works at scale: alumni first, then shared-background bankers, 10 to 15 personalized emails a week, 15-minute calls, always ending with "is there anyone else you'd suggest I speak with?" A referral does not get you the job; it gets your resume read by a human, which is the entire battle at a non-target.

The proof of work. Nothing separates candidates like unforced evidence: a stock pitch with a real model attached, a blog dissecting a deal, completing a curriculum like this one and being able to defend an LBO's math out loud. Interviewers forgive a missing brand name; they do not forgive missing preparation.

The boutique-to-bulge path is the classic non-target route: a no-name summer at a 4-person M&A shop, leveraged into junior-year interviews ("I closed a $30M deal where I built the model myself" beats "I fetched coffee at a famous logo"), then a lateral after a year. Recruiters see this pattern constantly and respect it; the industry is snobbish about entry but mercenary about competence.

09.6 The technical interview bank

Technicals test whether you can think in the language of Modules 03 to 06 under mild pressure. Work through these until the answers feel obvious; every one is a real interview staple.

Accounting

Walk me through the 3 financial statements.

The income statement measures profitability over a period: revenue down to net income. The cash flow statement starts from net income, adjusts for non-cash items and working capital, and adds investing and financing flows to show the change in cash. The balance sheet is the snapshot linking them: cash flows into the cash balance, net income (less dividends) rolls into retained earnings, and assets always equal liabilities plus equity.

Walk me through $10 of depreciation (40% of interviews ask exactly this).

Assume a 25% tax rate. Income statement: pre-tax income falls $10, taxes fall $2.50, net income falls $7.50. Cash flow statement: start with net income down $7.50, add back the $10 non-cash depreciation, so cash rises $2.50. Balance sheet: cash up $2.50, PP&E down $10, so assets down $7.50; retained earnings down $7.50 on the other side. It balances, and the punchline is that depreciation raises cash through the tax shield.

Why can a profitable company run out of cash?

Because accrual profits are not cash. A fast grower books revenue as receivables, builds inventory ahead of sales, and pays suppliers now: working capital soaks up cash even while the income statement smiles. Add capex and debt maturities and profitable companies die of illiquidity regularly. This is why lenders read the cash flow statement first.

What is working capital and why does it matter in deals?

Operating current assets minus operating current liabilities: roughly receivables plus inventory minus payables. It is the cash tied up in simply running the business. In M&A, purchase agreements set a normalized working capital "peg"; deliver less at close and the price adjusts against you, which is why unglamorous working capital fights are where real money moves.

EBITDA: why does everyone use it, and what is wrong with it?

It approximates operating cash generation before capital structure, taxes, and accounting depreciation choices, making companies comparable and multiples easy. What is wrong: it ignores capex (fatal for asset-heavy businesses), working capital swings, and stock comp if adjusted away. Famous critique: calling EBITDA a proxy for cash flow is how leveraged disasters get funded.

Valuation

What are the 3 main valuation methods, and which prints highest?

Trading comps (market prices for minority stakes), precedent transactions (real prices paid for control), and DCF (intrinsic value of forecast cash flows). Precedents usually print highest because they embed control premiums and synergies; the DCF can land anywhere because it obeys its assumptions; comps set the market baseline.

Enterprise value vs equity value, in one breath.

Equity value is the shareholders' claim; enterprise value is the whole operating business: equity plus net debt (plus preferred and minorities). EV pairs with pre-interest metrics like EBITDA; equity value pairs with net income. A company with $800M market cap, $300M debt, $100M cash has EV of $1,000M.

Two companies have identical EBITDA. Why might one trade at twice the multiple?

Because a multiple is compressed expectations: faster growth, better margins and cash conversion, stickier customers, lower risk, better management, scarcer asset. Saying "it trades at 14x vs 7x" is the market saying its future cash flows are worth twice as much per current dollar of EBITDA.

Walk me through a DCF.

Project unlevered free cash flow for 5 to 10 years (revenue growth, margins, taxes, capex, working capital). Discount those flows at WACC. Add a terminal value, either a Gordon growth perpetuity with g safely below WACC or an exit multiple, discounted back. The sum is enterprise value; subtract net debt for equity value; divide by shares for a per-share answer. Then, crucially, sensitize WACC and terminal assumptions, because the terminal usually carries most of the value.

Would raising cheap debt change a company's EV?

Not directly: EV measures the operating business, and swapping equity for debt just reallocates claims (equity value falls, debt rises, cash effects net out). Indirectly, yes, at the margins: the tax shield and discipline of debt can lower WACC and nudge intrinsic EV up until distress risk pushes back. The trap answer is "EV rises by the cash raised": it does not, cash is netted out.

M&A

When is a deal accretive vs dilutive?

Accretive when the acquisition adds more earnings than the cost of funding it: pro forma EPS rises. Quick screen for all-stock deals: if the acquirer's P/E exceeds the target's (post-premium), the deal is accretive, because you are paying with a currency the market values richer than what you are buying. Cash deals compare the target's earnings yield against the after-tax cost of the cash or debt used.

Why do most synergy estimates favor cost over revenue synergies?

Cost synergies (headcount overlap, procurement, facilities) are within management's control and reliably bankable, so buyers will pay for a portion of them. Revenue synergies require customers to behave as hoped, so boards discount them heavily, often to zero, in what they are willing to fund in the price.

What actually happens between signing and closing?

Regulatory clearances (antitrust, foreign investment), shareholder votes if required, financing funding, and the covenants that keep the seller running the business normally. The gap can run months; the merger agreement's conditions, MAC clause, and break fees govern who bears which risks if the world changes in between.

LBO

What makes a good LBO target?

Stable, predictable cash flows to service debt; low existing leverage; modest capex needs; defensible market position; costs to cut or add-ons to buy; a credible exit. Boring is beautiful: lenders and sponsors both underwrite predictability, not excitement.

Paper LBO: buy at 8x on $50M EBITDA, 60% debt. EBITDA reaches $70M in year 5, exit at 8x, $100M of debt paid down. Returns?

Entry: EV = 8 × 50 = $400M; debt $240M, equity $160M. Exit: EV = 8 × 70 = $560M; debt remaining 240 − 100 = $140M; equity = $420M. MOIC = 420 ÷ 160 = roughly 2.6x; over 5 years that is an IRR of about 21%. Say the steps out loud in that order and you have passed.

Why does leverage amplify equity returns?

The debt's claim is fixed, so every dollar of value created beyond interest flows entirely to a small equity slice. Buy $400M with $160M of equity and a $160M value gain doubles the equity rather than rising 40%. The same lever works in reverse, which is where Module 06's cautionary tales come from.

Judgment and market questions

"Pitch me a stock" (or a deal). What is the structure?

Four beats, 2 minutes: what it is (one sentence), why the market is wrong (the variant view, the heart of the pitch), what it is worth (a number with a method attached), and what proves you right or wrong (catalysts and the kill condition). The kill condition is what separates a thesis from a hope, and interviewers listen for it.

Rates just rose 200bps. Walk me through what happens to valuations.

Discount rates rise, so present values fall, hardest for long-duration cash flows: growth equity and long bonds. Multiples compress, LBO math strains as debt costs eat coverage, deal volume slows while bid-ask spreads between buyers and sellers reset. Connecting one macro input through the whole machine is exactly what this curriculum trains.

Interviewers are not grading trivia; they are checking whether the machine hangs together in your head. If you can walk depreciation through the statements, bridge EV to equity, and do a paper LBO with round numbers out loud, you are technically ready for almost any entry-level seat in the industry.

09.7 Offers, first years, and staying human

Choosing between offers and surviving year 1 are skills of their own, and the industry does not hand out a manual.

Evaluating offers: group and deal flow beat brand at the margin. An analyst on a busy team at a strong boutique often exits better than one parked on a quiet mega-bank desk, because the buy-side buys reps, not logos. Ask where the last 3 analyst classes went; firms that place well will tell you instantly, and the ones that dodge are answering too.

The first-year contract with yourself: reliability compounds faster than brilliance. Turning clean work on time, flagging problems early, and being pleasant at 1am is the entire job for 12 months; reputations set in the first 90 days and follow you between firms for a decade.

The honest part: burnout is real and structural, not a personal failing. The healthy operators treat banking years like an expedition: a defined window, a purpose (skills, savings, exits), non-negotiable anchors (sleep floors, one protected day), and a pre-decided exit condition. Leaving on plan is a win, not a defeat; the industry is a toolkit, and the tools stay with you wherever you take them.
