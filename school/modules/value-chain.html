Module 02 · Primer

The Industry Value Chain

Who does what, who pays whom, and how the whole machine fits together.

02.1 The map

Finance looks like chaos from the outside: thousands of firms, impenetrable job titles, everyone apparently rich. From above, it is one simple machine. Capital flows from people who have it to people who can use it, and every firm on the map is either moving that capital, advising on it, or building the rails it moves on. Learn the map once and every headline, job posting, and deal rumor snaps into place.

The map has four columns. On the far left sit the owners of capital: households, and the institutions that pool their savings. On the far right sit the users of capital: companies and governments that need money to build things. Everyone in between is an intermediary, and intermediaries come in two families. The buy-side invests other people's money for a fee. The sell-side sells services around securities: advice, execution, underwriting (buying a new issue of securities from the company and reselling it to investors). Section 02.2 draws that line precisely.

Underneath all of it runs the infrastructure: exchanges, clearinghouses, custodians, data vendors, rating agencies, and the regulators refereeing the whole game. You will rarely read a headline about a custodian. That is the point. Plumbing only makes news when it breaks.

Every product, every job title, and every acronym in finance sits downstream of some fee stream. When you meet a firm you do not understand, ask the mentor's question: who pays them, and for what?

02.2 Buy-side vs sell-side

The first question anyone in finance asks about a firm, a job, or a person: buy-side or sell-side? It is the industry's continental divide. The names confuse everyone at first, because both sides buy and sell securities all day long. The divide is about the business model, not the direction of the trades.

The sell-side creates, markets, and trades securities, and sells services around them: investment banks, brokerage arms, sales and trading desks, and the research departments attached to them. Their revenue is transactional: a fee when a deal closes, a spread when a client trades. The buy-side buys securities and whole companies with pooled client capital and charges for managing it: mutual funds, ETFs, hedge funds, private equity, venture capital. Their revenue is recurring: basis points on assets, plus a share of profits.

Ask who the client is and the divide gets sharper. A buy-side firm has one client, the asset owner whose money it manages, and one report card, returns net of fees. A sell-side firm serves two masters at once. Issuers pay it to place securities at the highest possible price. Investors, its other clients, want to buy those same securities at the lowest. Managing that tension (with disclosures, walls, and lawyers) is half of what a bank does all day.

The second divide is whose money is at risk. When a bank underwrites a stock offering, it commits its own balance sheet: it buys the entire issue from the company and resells it within hours. If the market gaps down in between, the bank eats the loss. Market-making desks likewise hold inventory that can move against them. The buy-side risks client capital instead; the firm's own money is mostly not on the table, though PE and hedge fund principals typically invest alongside their clients precisely so that it is, a little.

Pay follows the model. Sell-side compensation is salary plus an annual bonus keyed to fees generated or desk profits: steadier, paid yearly, sized by the fee pool. Buy-side pay is keyed to assets and performance: management fees fund the salaries, performance fees and carried interest fund the upside. Carry pays out over years, which is why private equity pay looks modest for a decade and then, sometimes, very much does not.

The names are not about buying vs selling securities. A sell-side market maker buys thousands of times a day; a hedge fund sells short constantly. The side refers to the flow of services: the sell-side sells research, execution, and deals to the buy-side, and the buy-side buys them with commissions and fees. Remember it this way: the buy-side is the customer.

02.3 Follow the fees

Nobody in the middle of the map works for free, and almost nobody sends an invoice either. Fees in finance are clipped: skimmed as percentages of assets, deals, spreads, and interest, usually invisible to the person ultimately paying. This section prices every arrow on the map, then follows a real-sized check through one full year to count the clips.

Start with the buy-side's fee, the management fee, quoted in basis points. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percentage point: 100 bps = 1%. Small numbers, vast bases:

Management fee = AUM x fee rate $100M x 0.50% (50 bps) = $500K per year, every year, regardless of performance

The rate depends on what you are buying. A passive index fund that merely tracks the market charges as little as 3 bps. An active mutual fund trying to beat the market charges 50-100 bps. Hedge funds layer a performance fee on top: the classic 2-and-20 (2% of assets plus 20% of profits), though allocator pressure has pulled the average closer to 1.5-and-15. Private equity charges roughly 2% on committed capital plus 20% carried interest on gains above a hurdle, usually 8%. The pattern: the more active, illiquid, and exclusive the strategy, the fatter the clip.

Deal fees work differently: no assets, just a success fee as a percentage of deal value, paid at closing. The grandfather of all fee schedules is the Lehman formula from the 1970s: 5% of the first $1M of deal value, 4% of the second, 3% of the third, 2% of the fourth, 1% of everything above. Inflation gutted the original numbers, and bankers now quote scaled or "double Lehman" variants, but the shape survives half a century later: the percentage falls as the deal grows, while the dollars rise. Illustrative only. Real fees are negotiated deal by deal: complexity, competition, and relationships all move them. Retainers are usually small and credited against the success fee.

Underwriting fees, called the gross spread, follow the same shape. A small-cap IPO still pays the famous 7% spread, split among the syndicate banks. Mega IPOs negotiate hard: 2-3% is typical, and the very largest go lower still. Investment grade bond deals run far cheaper, roughly 0.4-0.7%, because placing safe debt from a household name takes distribution, not persuasion.

Visa's IPO, March 2008. Visa went public weeks after Bear Stearns collapsed and still raised about $17.9B, the largest US listing ever at the time. The syndicate, led by JPMorgan, shared a gross spread of roughly 2.8%: about $500M of fees on a single transaction, split across dozens of banks by role, with the lead banks taking the largest slices. Nobody clips 7% on a deal that size. Nobody needs to.

Finally, the oldest clip in banking: lenders earn the spread between what their loans yield and what their funding costs, the net interest margin (NIM).

Net interest margin = yield on assets - cost of funding e.g. 6.5% earned on loans vs 2.5% paid on deposits = 4.0% NIM

$100M for one year. A pension hands $100M to the machine on a typical allocation: $40M passive index funds, $30M active funds, $20M private equity, $10M hedge funds. Count the clips over one year. The investment consultant who designed the allocation takes 5 bps ($50K). The custodian safekeeping everything takes 2 bps ($20K). The index funds take 3 bps on $40M ($12K). The active managers take 50 bps on $30M ($150K). The hedge fund takes 2% on $10M ($200K) plus 20% of an 8% gross gain ($160K). The PE fund takes 2% on $20M ($400K), with carried interest accruing toward exit on top (call it $200K in a decent year). Embedded trading costs inside all the portfolios (spreads and commissions) run about $50K. Cash fees: roughly $1.0M. All-in with accrued carry: about $1.2M, or 1.2%, in a year when nothing dramatic happened.

Is 1.2% a lot? In one year it looks like a rounding error. Compounded over 30 years, fee drag can consume a third of a portfolio's ending value, which is why Module 07's fee drag visualizer deserves 5 minutes of your time. It also explains the industry's deepest current: the multi-decade migration of money from active funds to passive index funds is a war fought over exactly this 1.2%.

02.4 Inside an investment bank

"Investment bank" names both a firm and a division inside it, which confuses everyone. The firm you read about in headlines is really 4 or 5 distinct businesses stapled together, selling different products to different clients under one brand. Here is the tour, floor by floor, and the wall that runs through the middle of the building.

Anatomy of a full-service investment bank

Five businesses, one brand, and a wall down the middle.

Boutique advisory firms are roughly the left half only; a pure asset manager is the bottom-right box grown into a whole company.

Inside the Investment Banking Division, bankers split two ways. Coverage bankers own relationships in a sector: the healthcare team knows every hospital-chain CEO and CFO, calls on them for years, and earns the right to pitch when something stirs. Product bankers own transaction types: M&A tactics, leveraged finance structures, equity offerings. A live deal staffs both: coverage brings the client, product executes. Managing directors sell, vice presidents manage, analysts and associates build the models at 1am. Each floor sells something different:

IBD: coverage and product groups

IBD sells advice and access to capital, and it gets paid almost entirely in contingent success fees: a percentage of the deal at closing, sized by the scale you played with in 02.3. That makes IBD a rainmaker economy. A single managing director with strong CEO relationships can generate $20M+ of annual fees, which is why senior bankers are paid like the franchises they are.

The rhythm of the floor follows the deal cycle: months of unpaid pitching (the "bake-off" decks), then a mandate, then a sprint to closing. Juniors live in the models and the data room; seniors live on planes.

ECM and DCM: the new-issue desks

Equity Capital Markets and Debt Capital Markets are the hinge between the bankers and the markets floor. When a company raises money, ECM or DCM prices the new securities, builds the order book, and allocates them to investors. Their pay comes out of the underwriting spread: on an IPO, the gross spread is carved into a management fee, an underwriting fee, and a selling concession, split across the syndicate by role.

Because they know the private deal but must talk to public investors to place it, ECM and DCM sit closest to the wall, and cross it only through scripted, compliance-monitored processes.

Sales & Trading: the market floor

S&T sells liquidity. Salespeople cover investor clients; traders quote two-way prices (a bid to buy, an ask to sell) so those clients can transact instantly, and the desk earns the spread between them plus commissions. Desks split into equities and FICC (fixed income, currencies, and commodities). The prime brokerage desk finances hedge funds, lending them money and stock, which is one of the steadiest profit pools in the building.

Since the Volcker rule (2010) restricted proprietary trading, the desks mostly trade client flow rather than pure house bets, but they still carry inventory, and inventory can go wrong in a hurry.

Equity Research: the public voice

Analysts publish ratings (buy, hold, sell), earnings estimates, and industry deep dives. Their estimates aggregate into the consensus that companies beat or miss every quarter. Almost nobody pays for research directly: historically it rode on trading, with clients rewarding good analysts by routing commission flow to the bank's desks.

Europe's MiFID II rules (2018) forced funds to pay for research explicitly rather than bundling it into commissions, and research budgets shrank sharply. The product survives because it still wins corporate access, supports new issues with aftermarket coverage, and keeps the bank's name in front of investors daily.

Asset & Wealth Management: the steady engine

The bank's own buy-side: funds it manages and advice it sells to wealthy families and institutions, charging basis points on AUM. It is the least glamorous floor and often the most valuable, because recurring fee revenue smooths the violent cyclicality of deals and trading. Markets pay higher multiples for revenue that shows up every year without a transaction attached.

The wall down the middle

IBD lives on material nonpublic information (MNPI): who is for sale, at what price, funded how. Anyone who trades public securities on that knowledge commits a crime. So banks build an information barrier, universally called the Chinese wall, between the private side (IBD, which knows things) and the public side (S&T, research, and asset management, which trade and publish). The wall is physical (separate floors, badge access), procedural (restricted lists, surveillance of chats and calls), and cultural: a banker "wall-crosses" a public-side colleague only through compliance, logged, for a defined purpose.

The Global Research Analyst Settlement, 2003. The wall got its modern enforcement teeth in April 2003, when ten Wall Street firms paid a combined $1.4B to settle charges that their analysts had publicly hyped dot-com stocks their banking colleagues were underwriting, while privately trashing them in emails that regulators gleefully published. The settlement forced structural separation: analyst pay cannot be tied to banking deals, bankers cannot review ratings, and every research report since carries pages of disclosures.

Full-service banks bundle all 5 floors; boutique advisory firms sell only the advice, arguing that a firm with no lending book, no trading desk, and no research to manage has fewer conflicts. Boards increasingly hire one of each on big deals: a full-service bank for the financing muscle, a boutique for the clean second opinion. Both fee checks clear.

02.5 The supporting cast

Look at the tombstone announcement of any large deal (the boxed notice listing everyone involved) and count the logos. Two banks, three law firms, two accounting firms, a consultancy, an insurer. Most of them are not "in finance" the way this module has used the word, and the deal literally cannot close without them.

Law firms

Every deal is ultimately a stack of documents, and lawyers write them: purchase agreements, credit agreements, prospectuses, antitrust filings. Top partners bill $2K+ per hour, and a large deal can generate $5M-15M in combined legal fees. M&A and capital markets work is Big Law's profit engine.

The Big 4 accountants

Audit is the recurring annuity; deals are the premium product. Transaction teams produce quality of earnings (QoE) reports that stress-test a target's numbers, plus tax structuring and purchase accounting. In diligence, the QoE is the document buyers trust over the audited statements.

Consultants

Strategy houses and specialist shops answer the question bankers cannot: is the market real? Commercial due diligence sizes demand, synergy studies validate the deal math, and post-merger integration teams try to make the combined company actually work. Fees run $500K to several million per study.

Fintech and market rails

Payment networks, order management systems, settlement rails, data pipes, and the unglamorous software the industry actually runs on (yes, Excel counts). Increasingly this layer is where the margin hides: software fees scale with volume and never sleep.

Financial media and data

Journalists, terminals, and newsletters set the shared facts and the narrative. A well-timed leak to a deals reporter is a negotiating tactic with its own etiquette, and an index provider's decision about what counts as "the market" moves more money than most analyst calls.

Placement agents, proxy advisors, IR

Placement agents raise funds for PE firms (roughly 2% of capital raised). Proxy advisors tell institutions how to vote their shares, which gives 2 firms outsized power over every merger vote. Investor relations firms manage the shareholder register between crises.

Why does every deal need 5 logos? Two forces. The first is specialization: each advisor answers one question no one else can answer as well. What is it worth? Is it legal? Are the numbers real? Is the market real? Who funds it? The second is liability transfer: a board that hired brand-name advisors, commissioned a QoE, and obtained a fairness opinion has a documented, defensible process if the deal later sours. Courts protect directors who took expert advice; shareholders sue the ones who winged it. A meaningful slice of every fee buys not analysis but insurance: for careers, for boards, against hindsight.

The Twitter buyout, 2022. Elon Musk's $44B take-private made fee math front-page news. Goldman Sachs, advising Twitter, was reported to earn roughly $80M with the bulk contingent on closing; JPMorgan roughly $53M on the same basis. When Musk spent the summer trying to walk away, the advisors' incentive to force a closing became a story in itself. Add Musk's own bankers, 2 armies of lawyers billing through the litigation, and about $13B of buyout debt that underwriting banks were stuck holding when markets turned, and one deal fed nearly every player in this module.

02.6 One deal through the machine

Time to run the whole machine at once, with fictional players. Meridian Packaging Group is a founder-owned maker of food-grade packaging: $250M of revenue, $50M of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, the standard cash-profit yardstick). The founder is 68 and ready. Cascade Industrial Holdings, a listed packaging group, will end up paying $500M, a 10.0x EBITDA multiple. More than a dozen organizations will touch the deal on its way to closing. Watch what each touch costs.

  1. Month 0 · The mandate
    The founder hires a banker
    Meridian's founder interviews 3 investment banks in a bake-off. Midmarket boutique Harborlight Partners wins the sell-side mandate: a $100K quarterly retainer credited against a success fee of roughly 1.25% of deal value, with everything beyond the retainer contingent on closing. From this moment, Harborlight earns only if the founder sells. Fee at stake: $6.3M.
  2. Months 1-2 · The package
    Building the story and scrubbing the numbers
    Harborlight's analysts build the model and the confidential information memorandum (CIM), the deal's marketing book. A Big 4 firm runs a sell-side quality of earnings study so buyers cannot later claim surprise ($750K). A virtual data room vendor hosts thousands of documents ($75K). Deal counsel starts drafting ($2.5M through close).
  3. Month 3 · The auction
    60 calls, 25 NDAs, 9 bids
    Harborlight quietly contacts 60 potential buyers: 40 strategics, 20 PE firms. 25 sign NDAs (confidentiality agreements) and receive the CIM. 9 submit first-round indications of interest (IOIs). The median IOI lands around 9.0x EBITDA; Cascade leads at 9.5x. Competition is the sell-side advisor's real product.
  4. Months 4-5 · Diligence
    The buyer builds its own machine
    Cascade assembles its advisor stack: buy-side bank Stonebriar Securities ($3.5M, mostly contingent), a strategy consultancy for commercial due diligence on packaging demand ($1.2M), and its own QoE team ($750K). After 6 weeks of management meetings, plant tours, and 400 data room questions, Cascade bids 10.0x: $500M. The auction's tension added half a turn of EBITDA.
  5. Month 6 · The financing
    $250M of new debt
    Cascade funds the deal with cash on hand plus a new $250M term loan. Two commercial banks underwrite it, charging about 2% in arrangement and underwriting fees ($5.0M). A rating agency rates the new debt ($150K, paid by the issuer, as always).
  6. Month 7 · Papers and insurance
    200 pages of who-bears-which-risk
    Lawyers on both sides grind out the stock purchase agreement (SPA): representations, warranties, indemnities. Cascade's counsel bills $3.0M. A representations and warranties insurance policy ($50M of coverage at a premium of roughly 2.8%, or $1.4M) replaces most of the escrow the founder would otherwise leave behind.
  7. Month 8 · The referees
    Filings and logistics
    The deal crosses regulatory desks: a Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust filing (about $250K) and its waiting period. An escrow and paying agent is appointed to move the money ($50K). Background checks, lien searches, and closing logistics round out roughly $75K of miscellany.
  8. Closing day
    The wires fly
    $500M moves from Cascade and its lenders, through the paying agent, to the founder and option holders. Harborlight's $6.3M success fee is wired from proceeds at the closing table, and every contingent fee on both sides crystallizes in the same hour. That evening, the closing dinner. The founder pays for nothing.

Was the friction worth it? Run the founder's math. The first-round median was 9.0x, or $450M. The auction Harborlight staged, the QoE that pre-empted price chips, and the insurance that freed his escrow delivered 10.0x with clean cash at close: call it $50M of price improvement against roughly $10M of sell-side costs (Cascade paid the other $15M and books it into the cost of the acquisition). The machine is expensive. It is usually cheaper than not using it.

And notice, one last time, where every dollar of that $25M came from and went. It came from the two ends of the map, a buyer and a seller of a real business. It went to the middle: 2 banks, 2 lenders, 4 professional firms, an insurer, a data room, a rating agency, and the government. That is the industry value chain, run once, end to end.
