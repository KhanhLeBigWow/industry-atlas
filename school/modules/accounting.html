Accounting & the Three Statements

Every model, every valuation, every deal argument starts here. The three statements are how a business describes itself in numbers, and fluency in them is the price of admission to the rest of finance.

03.1 The language of business

Warren Buffett calls accounting the language of business, and like any language it exists to answer questions. A company's financial statements answer exactly three: did we make money, what do we own and owe, and where did the cash actually go? One statement per question. Learn to read all three together and a company stops being a logo and becomes a machine you can inspect.

Income statement

Performance. Did we make money over a period (a quarter, a year)? Revenue at the top, costs subtracted in layers, profit at the bottom. It is a video of earning, not a photo of owning.

Balance sheet

Position. What do we own, what do we owe, and what is left for the shareholders, at a single instant? A snapshot taken at midnight on the last day of the period.

Cash flow statement

Cash. Where did money physically move? It reconciles accounting profit to the change in the bank account, and it is the hardest of the three to manipulate.

Why three documents instead of one? Because of a deliberate design choice called accrual accounting: companies record revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash changes hands. That single choice makes profit a better measure of economic performance, and it simultaneously makes profit different from cash. The cash flow statement exists to bridge that gap, and the balance sheet is where the timing differences (unpaid invoices, unsold inventory, unpaid bills) sit while they wait to become cash.

A vivid example. Alpine Plow Co., a fictional snow-removal outfit, signs a $90K winter contract. It buys $30K of salt for cash in November, does all the plowing in December, invoices on December 31, and collects in February. Ask "how did December go?" and the two accounting views give wildly different answers:

ViewNovemberDecemberFebruary
Cash view (money in and out)($30K)$0$90K
Accrual view (profit earned)$0$60K$0

The cash view says December, the month of all the actual work, was a non-event, and February, a month of doing nothing, was a triumph. The accrual view tells the economic truth: the business earned $60K in December ($90K of revenue against the $30K of salt it consumed). The salt purchase in November was not an expense when it was bought; it became inventory, an asset, and turned into an expense only when it was used. Collecting the invoice in February creates no new profit; it just converts one asset (a receivable) into another (cash).

Revenue is an opinion, cash is a fact. Accrual profit involves judgment calls (when is revenue earned? how fast do machines depreciate?), while the movement of cash is close to indisputable. That is why every serious analysis reads the income statement and the cash flow statement side by side, and treats a persistent gap between them as a question demanding an answer.

Where you find these documents: US public companies file audited annual statements in the 10-K and unaudited quarterly ones in the 10-Q, both free on the SEC's EDGAR site. The statements themselves take minutes to read. The footnotes behind them, where the judgment calls are disclosed, are where professionals spend their time.

The income statement is a controlled descent from revenue to profit, with each line subtracting a different category of cost. Walk the descent. Revenue (the top line) is the value of goods and services delivered during the period, recognized when earned. Cost of goods sold (COGS) is what it directly cost to make what was sold: Meridian's steel, castings, and factory labor. What remains is gross profit, and gross margin (40% here) measures the raw economics of the product itself, before the cost of running the company around it.

Next comes operating expense (opex): selling, general and administrative costs (SG&A), research and development, the salespeople, the headquarters, the ERP system. These support the business as a whole rather than any single unit sold. Subtracting them leaves operating profit before depreciation, which the industry calls EBITDA.

Depreciation and amortization (D&A) deserves a pause because it confuses everyone at first. When Meridian buys a $50M production line, accrual accounting refuses to expense it all at once, since the machine will produce for 10 years. Instead the cost is spread: $5M of depreciation per year. D&A is therefore a real expense with no cash attached; the cash left in year one, and the income statement feels it gradually. Amortization is the same idea applied to intangible assets such as acquired customer relationships or software.

After D&A comes EBIT, or operating income: profit from actually running the business, before anyone asks how it is financed. Then the capital structure enters. Interest expense ($24M here, on $400M of debt at 6%) compensates lenders. Taxes take their statutory share of what is left (we use a flat 25% throughout this module). The residual, net income, belongs to shareholders, which is why per-share metrics (EPS) and the P/E ratio are built on it.

EBITDA = Net income + Taxes + Interest + D&A   (equivalently: EBIT + D&A)

An honest word on EBITDA, because you will hear it constantly. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization is meant to approximate the operating cash engine of the business, stripped of financing choices (interest), jurisdiction (taxes), and historical capex (D&A). It is useful precisely because it ignores those things: it lets you compare two identical factories even if one is leveraged and one is not, and it is the denominator of the debt market's favorite ratio, net debt to EBITDA. Sellers and lenders quote it, and buyout math practically runs on it.

EBITDA is not cash flow, and treating it as cash flow is one of the classic ways smart people lose money. It ignores capital expenditure (Meridian must spend roughly $55M a year on equipment just to stay competitive), ignores cash absorbed by working capital as the business grows, and ignores the fact that interest and taxes must be paid in actual dollars. A capital-intensive company can post fat EBITDA and still generate almost no free cash. Charlie Munger liked to say that every time you hear EBITDA you should substitute a much ruder phrase.

03.3 The balance sheet: a snapshot that always balances

If the income statement is a video of the period, the balance sheet is a photograph taken at the final midnight. It lists everything the company owns (assets), everything it owes (liabilities), and the residual claim belonging to shareholders (equity). One identity governs the whole document, and it is true by construction, not by luck.

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity

The logic is simple: everything a company owns was paid for with somebody's money, either borrowed money (liabilities) or the owners' money (equity, both what they invested and the profits retained over the years). Equity is defined as whatever makes the equation hold, which is why it is called the residual claim. If assets rise and liabilities do not, the owners got richer. Every transaction in accounting touches at least two accounts precisely so that this identity survives, which is what "double-entry bookkeeping" means.

The dashed region matters more than it looks. Working capital, current assets minus current liabilities, is the money tied up in the everyday plumbing of the business: inventory sitting in the warehouse, invoices customers have not paid yet, minus the bills Meridian itself has not paid. It is capital the company must fund but that earns nothing sitting there. Watch it closely: when a company grows, working capital usually grows with it, silently consuming cash. Section 03.4 shows how that consumption can kill a profitable company.

Working capital = Current assets - Current liabilities

One more idea before the line items: book value is not market value. Meridian's equity is carried at $250M, but that number is an accounting residual built on historical costs. The stock market might value the same equity at $600M because it prices future profits, not past transactions. The balance sheet records history; markets price the future. The gap between the two is where valuation (Module 04) lives.

The vocabulary you will meet on nearly every balance sheet:

Cash and equivalents

Actual money plus anything convertible to money within about 90 days (money market funds, T-bills). The most honest line on the statement, and the first thing a lender checks. Analysts usually net it against debt to compute net debt.

Accounts receivable

Money customers owe for goods already delivered. Revenue has been recognized; cash has not arrived. Measured in days ("DSO", days sales outstanding), it tells you how fast customers pay. Receivables growing much faster than revenue is a classic warning sign (see 03.7).

Inventory

Raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods waiting to be sold, carried at cost. Inventory is an expense-in-waiting: it moves to COGS only when the product ships. Bloated inventory means cash trapped in the warehouse and possible write-downs ahead.

Property, plant and equipment (PP&E)

Land, buildings, machines. Shown net: original cost minus accumulated depreciation. Meridian's $380M net PP&E might be $700M of historical purchases less $320M of depreciation taken so far. Capex adds to it; depreciation grinds it down.

Goodwill and intangibles

Goodwill appears only after acquisitions: it is the premium paid above the fair value of the target's identifiable net assets. It is not amortized but tested annually for impairment; a goodwill write-down is management publicly admitting it overpaid. Other intangibles (patents, brands, customer lists) amortize over their useful lives.

Accounts payable

Bills the company owes suppliers for goods already received. Payables are free financing: every extra day Meridian takes to pay is a day it holds the supplier's cash. Stretching payables boosts cash flow once, not forever, and suppliers eventually push back.

Accrued expenses and deferred revenue

Accrued expenses are costs incurred but not yet billed (wages earned this week, paid next month). Deferred revenue is cash collected before the work is done (a 12-month software subscription paid upfront); it is a liability because the company still owes the service. Counterintuitively, growing deferred revenue is usually great news: customers are prepaying.

Debt, current and long-term

Borrowed money with a contractual claim: revolvers, term loans, bonds. The portion due within 12 months sits in current liabilities. Unlike equity, debt has a maturity date and a coupon, and missing either hands control to the lenders. Leverage ratios (03.6) measure how heavy this burden is.

Shareholders' equity and retained earnings

Paid-in capital (what shareholders contributed) plus retained earnings (every dollar of net income ever earned, minus every dividend ever paid, accumulated over the company's life). Retained earnings is the hinge that connects the income statement to the balance sheet: this period's net income flows into it.

03.4 The cash flow statement: the lie detector

Accrual accounting is powerful and manipulable in equal measure, so the third statement follows the one thing that cannot be conjured: cash. The cash flow statement starts from net income, unwinds every accrual judgment, and reconciles all the way to the change in the bank balance. It is organized into three sections, and the pattern across them tells you what kind of company you are looking at.

Operating (CFO)

Cash from actually running the business: collecting from customers, paying suppliers and staff and taxes. The engine. Mature healthy companies generate strongly positive CFO, period after period.

Investing (CFI)

Cash spent on the future: capital expenditure, acquisitions, minus asset sales. Usually negative, and that is fine; a growing company should be planting seeds. The question is whether operations fund the planting.

Financing (CFF)

Cash to and from capital providers: debt raised or repaid, shares issued or bought back, dividends. Tells you whether the company is drawing fuel from outside or returning it.

Net change in cash = CFO + CFI + CFF

Nearly every company builds the operating section with the indirect method: begin with net income, then adjust. Add back expenses that consumed no cash (D&A is the big one). Then correct for working capital: subtract growth in receivables (sales booked but not collected) and inventory (cash converted into boxes), add growth in payables (bills you have not paid yet, meaning suppliers are lending to you). Now the punchline of this section: profitable companies can die. Bankruptcy is not triggered by losses on an income statement; it is triggered by an empty bank account on payroll day. And the fastest route to an empty bank account, strangely, is rapid profitable growth, because growth demands working capital upfront while profits arrive on paper first and in cash much later.

Juniper Beverage Co. grows itself into a corner. Juniper, a fictional drinks brand, doubles revenue from $20M to $40M in a year and earns an 8% net margin, so net income is a healthy $3.2M. But its grocery-chain customers pay in 60 days, it holds 90 days of inventory, and its suppliers demand payment in 30. Doubling the business doubles all of that plumbing: receivables climb $3.3M, inventory climbs $3.2M, and payables help by only $1.0M. Run the reconciliation: $3.2M net income + $0.8M of D&A ($3.3M) receivables build ($3.2M) inventory build + $1.0M payables = ($1.5M) of operating cash flow. Add $1.0M of capex for a new filling line and Juniper burned ($2.5M) of cash in its best year ever. Record profits, empty account. Unless it raises debt or equity, next year's growth is unfundable. This is why bankers say growth eats cash, and why working capital terms (how fast you collect versus how fast you pay) are fought over in every deal.

The reverse pattern is just as informative. Some businesses collect cash before they owe anyone anything: an insurer collects premiums years before paying claims, a software firm collects annual subscriptions upfront, a supermarket sells inventory for cash weeks before paying its suppliers. These companies enjoy negative working capital, meaning growth generates cash. When you hear investors describe a business model as beautiful, this is often what they mean.

03.5 How the three statements link

The statements are not three documents; they are one machine viewed from three angles, bolted together at three joints. Net income closes into retained earnings on the balance sheet. The same net income opens the cash flow statement. And the cash flow statement's ending cash is the cash line on the balance sheet. Push on any line and the ripple must travel through all three, or the books do not balance.

This is also the most reliable interview topic in finance. "Walk me through $100 of depreciation" has opened analyst interviews for decades, not because the math is hard, but because it instantly reveals whether you understand the machine or memorized the vocabulary. 03.6 Ratios: turning statements into judgment

Raw financial statements answer "what happened." Ratios answer "so what?" Each one is a question posed to the numbers: is the product profitable, can the company pay its bills this year, how heavy is the debt, and how hard is the owners' money working? Three habits separate people who use ratios well from people who recite them. First, compare against peers and against the company's own history; 2.0x leverage is sleepy for a utility and terrifying for a game studio. Second, mind the denominator: EBITDA-based ratios inherit every flaw of EBITDA. Third, never read one ratio alone. Meridian's DSO of 45 days (receivables of $105M against $850M of sales), inventory days of 79, and payable days of 61 combine into a 63-day cash conversion cycle, and it is the combination, not any single number, that describes the working capital machine.

Why professionals prefer ROIC to ROE: leverage flatters ROE without creating a single dollar of operating value. Meridian earns 31.2% on equity partly because its $800M asset base is financed with only $250M of equity. Refinance to double the debt and halve the equity, and ROE roughly doubles while the pumps and compressors earn exactly what they earned before. ROIC (14.8% here) charges the business for all capital used, debt and equity alike, so it cannot be dressed up with borrowing. High-ROIC businesses compound; high-ROE, low-ROIC businesses are often just leveraged.

03.7 Red flags: when the numbers lie

Accrual accounting hands management a set of dials: when to recognize revenue, what to capitalize, how fast to depreciate, what to call "one-time." Most teams use the dials honestly. Some lean on them, and a few break them off. Earnings quality is the discipline of asking how much of reported profit is real, recurring, and backed by cash.

Aggressive revenue recognition

Booking revenue before it is truly earned: recognizing a 3-year contract upfront, shipping to a warehouse and calling it sold, or "bill and hold" arrangements where goods never leave the dock. The tell: revenue grows, receivables grow faster, and DSO creeps relentlessly upward.

Capitalizing operating costs

Recording an everyday expense as an asset so it hits the income statement over years instead of today. Profit jumps instantly; nothing about the business improved. The tell: capex or "other assets" ballooning while reported margins mysteriously outrun every peer's.

Channel stuffing

Cramming product into distributors at quarter-end, with discounts or generous return rights, to borrow next quarter's sales. It works until the channel is full, then revenue falls off a cliff. The tell: quarter-end sales spikes, rising returns, and distributors' inventories swelling.

Serial "one-time" charges

Restructuring costs, impairments, and adjustments presented as exceptional, every single year. Each exclusion nudges "adjusted" earnings above reality. The tell: a decade of "non-recurring" items that somehow always recur, and an ever-widening gap between adjusted and GAAP profit.

The universal detector is the one you already own: compare net income to operating cash flow over multiple years. Accruals can be stretched for a quarter or four, but cash eventually refuses to cooperate. A company reporting rising profits while CFO stagnates or turns negative is either growing very fast and hungry for working capital (Juniper in 03.4, a legitimate story you can verify) or manufacturing earnings (a story you cannot). Rising DSO, swelling inventory, growing gaps between adjusted and GAAP numbers, and frequent auditor changes tell you which story you are in.

WorldCom, 2002. The telecom giant executed the capitalization trick at industrial scale: roughly $3.8B of ordinary network line costs (fees paid to other carriers, plainly an operating expense) were booked as capital expenditure, initially, with total misstatements eventually put at about $11B. The effect was mechanical: expenses vanished from the income statement and reappeared as assets, turning losses into reported profits while cash told the truth the whole time. An internal auditor, Cynthia Cooper, unraveled it; the company filed what was then the largest bankruptcy in US history in July 2002, months after Enron's 2001 collapse (hidden liabilities in off-balance-sheet entities and aggressive mark-to-market gains). Congress responded with the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, which is why CEOs and CFOs now personally certify their financial statements.

The professional's posture is skepticism without cynicism. Most statements are honest. But every fraud in the record books was visible in advance to readers who checked whether profits were becoming cash, and every one punished readers who stopped at the income statement.
