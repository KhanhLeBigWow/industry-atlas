Module 04 · Core

Valuation

Every deal, every pitch, every investment memo ends at the same question: what is this business worth? Professionals answer it with 3 lenses, and the craft is knowing what each lens distorts.

04.1 Two numbers people confuse: enterprise value and equity value

Before valuing anything, you must be precise about what you are valuing: the whole business, or just the owners' slice of it. Mixing the two is the most common technical error in finance interviews and, embarrassingly often, in real analysis.

Equity value (market capitalization for a public company) is what the shareholders' claim is worth. Enterprise value (EV, sometimes TEV) is what the entire operating business is worth to all capital providers together: shareholders and lenders alike. Since lenders have a claim that must be settled, buying the whole business means buying the equity and inheriting the net debt.

Enterprise value = Equity value + Total debt + Preferred + Minority interest − Cash (cash subtracts because a buyer pockets it at close: it is negative debt)

The EV bridge: Meridian Industrial Co.

From what shareholders own to what the whole business costs, $M.

Meridian's shareholders own $1,500M of value, but a buyer of the whole company effectively pays $1,860M once debt is assumed and cash is credited.

The pairing rule that follows: match the value measure to the cash flow measure. EBITDA and revenue belong to the whole enterprise (they are earned before lenders are paid), so they pair with EV. Net income and EPS belong to shareholders alone (interest is already deducted), so they pair with equity value.

EV/Net income and Price/EBITDA are the classic mismatch errors: they compare a whole-company number with a shareholders-only number. Two companies with identical businesses but different debt loads will look wildly different through a mismatched multiple, and identically through a matched one. Interviewers set this trap on purpose.

04.2 Relative valuation: multiples and trading comps

A multiple is a price tag standardized by size: dollars of value per dollar of earnings. Comparable company analysis ("trading comps") reads those price tags off similar public companies and applies them to yours.

Multiples look like shortcuts, and they are, but they are principled shortcuts: every multiple is a compressed DCF. A company deserves a higher EV/EBITDA when its cash flows grow faster, convert more reliably, or carry less risk. When analysts say "Northgate trades at 10x because of its growth," they are speaking DCF in shorthand.

Running comps is a craft in 3 steps: pick genuinely similar companies (industry, size, growth, margins), scrub their numbers to a common basis (calendarize fiscal years, strip one-time items), then decide where in the peer range your target deserves to sit and why. Comps tell you what the market pays today for businesses like yours. That is their power (real, observable prices) and their weakness (if the whole sector is euphoric or depressed, comps import the mood). Multiples are mirrors, not scales.

04.3 Intrinsic valuation: the DCF from first principles

A business is worth the cash it will hand its owners over its lifetime, discounted back to today. The discounted cash flow model takes that sentence literally, one assumption at a time.

The unlevered DCF values the whole enterprise, so it uses cash flows before any payments to lenders, discounted at a blended rate that reflects all capital providers. Three ingredients:

1. Free cash flow: what the business actually throws off

2. WACC: the blended price of the money

Future cash flows are discounted at the weighted average cost of capital: what equity holders demand, blended with what lenders charge, weighted by the capital mix. Debt is cheaper than equity (safer claim, plus interest is tax-deductible), so leverage pulls WACC down, until the added risk starts pushing both costs up.

WACC = (E/V) × Re + (D/V) × Rd × (1 − t) Re (cost of equity) = risk-free rate + β × equity risk premium [CAPM] Rd (cost of debt) = yield the company's lenders currently demand

3. Terminal value: everything after the forecast

You can forecast 5 years with a straight face, not 50. The terminal value compresses everything beyond the horizon into one number, computed either as a perpetuity growing at a modest rate g, or as an exit multiple on the final year's EBITDA. Professionals compute both and check them against each other.

Gordon growth: TV = FCF₅ × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g) g must be < WACC Exit multiple: TV = EBITDA₅ × chosen EV/EBITDA

Terminal value routinely carries 60% to 80% of a DCF's total. That means most of your "intrinsic" valuation lives in the least knowable inputs: g and the far-future WACC. A DCF is not wrong because of this, but anyone quoting one to the third decimal place is performing precision, not achieving it.

Try this experiment: set WACC to 9% and move terminal growth from 2% to 3%. EV jumps by roughly a fifth. Now set terminal growth back and move revenue growth from 8% to 9%. The move is far smaller. One point of forever-growth is worth more than one point of near-term growth, which is why terminal assumptions are where valuation fights actually happen.

04.5 Precedent transactions: what buyers actually paid

The third lens looks at real M&A: what did acquirers pay for whole companies like this one? Those prices run systematically higher than trading comps, and the gap has a name.

The control premium. A share price values a passive minority stake; an acquirer buys the right to run the company: replace management, cut costs, redirect cash, merge operations. That control, plus expected synergies, is worth paying up for. US public-company acquisitions have historically closed at premiums of roughly 20% to 40% over the undisturbed share price, higher in competitive auctions.

Precedents come with their own traps: deal terms hide earnouts and assumed liabilities, cycles matter enormously (2007 prices tell you little about 2009), and every printed multiple includes synergies specific to that buyer. Good analysts date-stamp every precedent and note the strategic context before citing it.

Where do these numbers come from? Public deal terms live in merger proxies and 8-K filings; databases like Refinitiv, CapIQ, and MergerMarket aggregate them; and for private deals, bankers trade intelligence the old-fashioned way. Half the value of a senior M&A banker is a mental database of what things actually cleared at.

04.6 The football field: putting it all together

No method gets the final word. The standard summary chart lines up every method's range so a decision-maker can see where they agree, and negotiate inside the overlap.

Each bar is one method's plausible range; the marker is a hypothetical $1,900M offer.

Reading it like a banker: the offer clears the trading range and comps, sits in the upper half of DCF, and inside precedents. A board can defend accepting it; a motivated seller can still argue for more.

The football field is also a negotiation map. Sellers anchor on the methods printing highest (precedents, optimistic DCF); buyers anchor low (trading comps, conservative DCF). The final price is less a discovery of truth than a settlement between defensible stories, which is why owning the analysis behind each bar is real leverage.

04.7 How valuations go wrong

The math is easy; the failure modes are behavioral. The classics, so you can spot them in the wild and in the mirror.

The hockey stick

Forecasts where growth conveniently accelerates right after the historical data ends. If years 1 to 3 of history grew 4% and the model shows 12% by year 5, someone is selling something.

Terminal value doing all the work

When 85% of EV sits in the terminal, the 5-year model is decoration. Check the terminal-value share (the sandbox reports it) before trusting any DCF.

Cherry-picked peers

Comps ranges move dramatically based on who makes the list. The peer set is the argument; the median is just arithmetic.

False precision

WACC estimated to 2 decimals from inputs that are themselves guesses. Sensitivity tables exist precisely because point estimates lie about certainty.

In the 2013 Dell buyout litigation, the Delaware court's own DCF later suggested a fair value about 28% above the $13.75 per share deal price, roughly $17.62, while other analyses defended the deal price. Same company, same filings, wildly different answers, hundreds of millions at stake. Valuation is a discipline of ranges and arguments, not a calculator with one right output. Courts, boards, and markets all know it.
