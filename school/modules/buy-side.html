Module 07 · Core

Asset Management & Hedge Funds

Wall Street's noisiest players are not its biggest. Meet the institutions that actually own the market, the fees they charge, and the quiet arithmetic that decides who keeps the returns.

07.1 Who owns the market

Turn on financial television and you will see traders. But trading is not owning. The true owners of the world's stocks and bonds are pension funds, mutual funds, ETFs, insurers, and sovereign funds, running other people's money at a scale no individual can approach. This section maps who they are, and how the map got redrawn over 70 years.

In the 1950s, the US stock market belonged to households. By most estimates, individuals held over 90% of US equities directly, in their own names, one certificate at a time. A broker called you about a stock, you bought 100 shares, and you were the shareholder of record. That world is gone. Today direct household holdings are well under half of the market, and the majority of equity is held by institutions: pooled vehicles investing on behalf of millions of underlying savers.

Three forces drove the shift. First, retirement saving was institutionalized: pension plans and later 401(k) accounts channeled a generation of paychecks into professionally managed pools. Second, pooled funds solved two problems the individual investor could not: diversification at low cost and access to professional management. Third, scale fed on itself. Bigger pools negotiated lower trading costs and lower fees, which attracted more assets, which lowered costs again.

A definition worth locking in now: the buy side is everyone whose job is to invest capital (asset managers, hedge funds, pensions, endowments, sovereign funds). The sell side (investment banks, brokers, dealers) exists to serve them: raising capital, executing trades, providing research and advice. Modules 02 and 05 covered the sell side's business. This module is about its clients.

The organizing idea for this whole module: follow the liabilities. Every pool of capital invests the way its obligations demand. A pension owes retirees monthly checks for decades, so it can hold illiquid assets. An insurer owes claims that arrive unpredictably, so it holds bonds it can sell or match. Once you know what a pool of money has promised, you can usually guess its portfolio.

07.2 Asset managers: mutual funds, ETFs, and the index revolution

An asset manager runs other people's money for a fee and never owns the assets it manages. Its product is a wrapper called a fund, and the two wrappers that conquered the world are the mutual fund and the ETF. The fee war between active and passive management inside those wrappers is the defining industry story of the last 30 years.

The business model is beautifully simple: revenue equals assets under management (AUM) times the fee rate. Costs are mostly people and technology, and they grow far slower than assets do. A firm managing $10B at a 0.5% fee earns $50M a year; double the AUM and revenue doubles while costs barely move. This is why asset gathering, not investment brilliance, is the commercial heart of the industry, and why distribution (getting your fund onto retirement menus and advisor platforms) matters as much as performance.

The mutual fund

An open-end mutual fund pools investor cash and issues shares priced once per day at net asset value (NAV): total assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. You buy and redeem directly with the fund itself, always at that day's closing NAV. The structure is simple and battle-tested, but it has quirks: the manager must hold some cash to meet redemptions (a drag on returns), and when the fund sells winners to pay departing investors, remaining shareholders can receive taxable capital gains distributions they never asked for.

The ETF

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) holds a portfolio just like a mutual fund but trades on an exchange all day like a stock. Its market price is set by supply and demand, not by the fund. What stops the price from drifting away from the value of the holdings is a mechanism most investors never see, and it is worth 3 minutes to understand properly.

How ETF creation and redemption actually works

ETFs do not sell shares to the public. They deal only with authorized participants (APs), large trading firms with a standing agreement with the fund. The loop works like this:

  1. Creation. When an ETF trades above the value of its holdings (its NAV), an AP buys the underlying basket of stocks in the market, delivers it to the fund in kind, and receives newly created ETF shares in return. The AP sells those shares at the rich price and pockets the difference.
  2. Redemption. When the ETF trades below NAV, the AP does the reverse: buys cheap ETF shares, hands them to the fund, and receives the basket of underlying stocks, which it sells at full value.
  3. The result. This arbitrage is riskless enough that APs compete to do it instantly, which pins the ETF's price to its NAV within pennies for liquid funds. Nobody does it out of civic duty; the profit motive is the glue.

The in-kind exchange has a famous side effect in the US: because the fund hands out securities rather than selling them, it rarely realizes taxable gains internally. That is the main reason ETFs are more tax-efficient than otherwise identical mutual funds, and a large part of why flows have migrated to the wrapper.

Active vs passive, and the revolution

Active management tries to beat a benchmark by picking securities or timing markets. Passive management tries to be the benchmark: hold every stock in an index at market weights, trade almost never, and charge almost nothing. The case for passive rests on an arithmetic fact, not an opinion: all investors together hold the market, so the average actively managed dollar earns the market return before fees, and less than the market return after fees. Skilled managers exist, but for every winner there must be a loser on the other side of the trade, and both pay their managers.

Investors took decades to accept this, then moved all at once.

In 1976 John Bogle launched the First Index Investment Trust, the first index mutual fund available to the public. Underwriters hoped to raise roughly $150M; the offering brought in about $11M, and competitors mocked it as "Bogle's folly." The fund, which became the Vanguard 500 Index Fund, proved the arithmetic over time: matching the market at near-zero cost beats most managers who try to beat it. Vanguard's unusual mutual ownership structure (the funds' shareholders effectively own the management company) let it keep cutting fees as it grew, and by the mid-2020s the firm managed roughly $10T. The product nobody wanted in 1976 became the default way Americans own stocks.

07.3 The allocators: pensions, endowments, sovereign funds, insurers

Above the asset managers sit the allocators: the institutions that actually own the capital and hire managers to run it. Asset managers compete for their mandates the way contractors bid for buildings. To understand an allocator, start with its liabilities, because a portfolio is always shaped by the promises it has to keep.

Pensions: two very different animals

Defined benefit (DB)

The employer promises a specific retirement check ("60% of final salary for life") and must invest to fund it. The plan bears the investment risk: if returns fall short, the sponsor tops up the gap. DB plans obsess over funded status (assets divided by the present value of promised benefits) and increasingly practice liability-driven investing (LDI): holding long-dated bonds that move with the value of the liabilities, so the gap stays stable even when markets swing.

Defined contribution (DC)

The employer promises only a contribution ("we match 4% of salary"), and the employee bears the investment risk in an individual account, the 401(k) being the classic example. The plan sponsor's job shrinks to picking the fund menu and the default option. That default, usually a target-date fund that automatically de-risks as retirement approaches, quietly became one of the largest pools of equity demand in the world.

The great pension story of the last 40 years is the migration from DB to DC in the private sector: risk moved from corporate balance sheets onto household ones, one plan freeze at a time. Public-sector plans remain mostly DB, which is why state pension funding debates never leave the news.

Endowments and the Yale model

University endowments and foundations are perpetual pools: they exist forever, spend roughly 5% of assets a year, and answer to no depositor who can demand cash tomorrow. That makes them the purest long-horizon investors in the system, and one of them rewrote the playbook.

When David Swensen took over Yale's endowment in 1985, it held roughly $1B in a conventional stock and bond mix. He rebuilt it around one insight: a perpetual institution needs no daily liquidity, so it should harvest the extra return that illiquid assets pay. Yale shifted heavily into private equity, venture capital, real assets, and absolute return strategies, keeping only a sliver in US public equities, and compounded at roughly 13% a year for decades; the endowment reached roughly $40B by 2021. The approach became famous as the Yale model and was copied by institutions everywhere. The copycats learned a hard lesson in 2008: the model demands iron liquidity planning, because private funds keep calling committed capital even when public markets freeze and donations dry up.

Sovereign wealth funds

A sovereign wealth fund (SWF) is a state-owned investment pool, typically built from commodity revenue or accumulated foreign exchange reserves. Some are stabilization funds, parking money to smooth government budgets through oil price swings; others are savings funds converting a finite resource into a permanent income stream for future generations. Norway's oil fund alone owns a small slice of nearly every listed company on earth, and Gulf and Asian funds have become some of the most important checks in private markets. Their horizon is generational, their liabilities political rather than contractual.

Insurance general accounts

Insurers collect premiums today and pay claims later, investing the float in between. The general account (the insurer's own balance sheet portfolio) is dominated by investment-grade bonds chosen to match the timing of expected claims, because regulation charges insurers more capital for holding risky assets. Life insurers, whose liabilities stretch decades, are the natural buyers of the longest corporate bonds and, increasingly, of private credit. It is unglamorous and enormous.

Mandates, the IPS, and the gatekeepers

When an allocator hires a manager, it awards a mandate: a contractual assignment to run a specific pool in a specific style ("$200M in US small-cap value against the Russell 2000 Value index, tracking error under 5%"). The rules of the whole portfolio live in the investment policy statement (IPS): a board-approved document stating objectives, risk tolerance, allowed asset classes, target allocation and ranges, benchmarks, and spending or liquidity rules. The IPS is the constitution; committees and managers operate inside it.

Between allocators and managers stand the investment consultants, hired by boards to recommend allocations and maintain approved manager lists. A small number of consulting firms advise on trillions of dollars, which makes them the industry's quiet gatekeepers: an asset manager who cannot get rated by the big consultants is invisible to much of the pension world. Critics note the incentive problem: consultants are rarely fired for recommending famous names, so safe, conventional advice dominates. Keep that thought; it returns in section 07.6.

07.4 Hedge funds: freedom, leverage, and 2 and 20

A hedge fund is defined not by what it trades but by what it is allowed to do. Strip away the mystique and you find a lightly regulated private fund with the freedom to short, to lever, to concentrate, and to charge performance fees. Some hedge. Many do not. The name survives mostly as a legal and fee structure.

Mandate freedom

A mutual fund must follow its prospectus: long-only, diversified, fully invested. A hedge fund's private placement documents allow almost anything: any asset class, any concentration, any direction. The manager sells judgment, unconstrained.

Shorting and leverage

Hedge funds can short (borrow a security, sell it, and profit if it falls) and can amplify positions with borrowed money. Both expand the toolkit and both cut in reverse: a short position's loss is theoretically unlimited, and leverage turns small errors into fatal ones.

The fee model

The classic terms are 2 and 20: a 2% annual management fee on assets plus 20% of profits. A high-water mark means performance fees apply only to gains above the fund's previous peak, so investors do not pay twice for the same recovery. Some funds add a hurdle rate: no performance fee until returns clear a stated minimum.

Structure and liquidity

Legally, a hedge fund is a private partnership: investors are limited partners, the manager the general partner. Only institutions and wealthy (accredited or qualified) investors may buy in. Capital is sticky by design: lockups bar withdrawals for a period, and gates can slow redemptions in a crisis.

Two exposure numbers describe any long/short book. Add the long and short positions and you get gross exposure, a measure of how much total risk and leverage the fund is running. Subtract them and you get net exposure, a measure of how much the fund still cares which way the market goes.

That is the original meaning of "hedged": the market component cancels, leaving only the manager's stock selection. Run the three scenarios and notice the net P&L never changes. The fund gave up the market's upside to buy immunity from its downside, and what it kept is called alpha: return that comes from skill rather than market direction.

The strategy map

The industry sorts itself into a handful of broad strategy families. The tabs below give you the elevator version of each, with a sketch of what the exposure or payoff looks like.

The original strategy, invented in 1949 when a journalist turned money manager first paired longs against shorts. Buy the stocks you believe in, short the ones you do not, and let the market exposure partially cancel. Most funds run net long (say 100% long against 60% short), keeping some market upside while cushioning drawdowns. The product is stock picking with the market turned down, not off, and returns live or die on the spread between the longs and the shorts.

Illustrative book: 100% long and 80% short leaves 20% net market exposure.

Event-driven funds invest around corporate events: mergers, spinoffs, restructurings, bankruptcies. The classic trade is merger arbitrage: after a deal is announced, the target trades slightly below the offer price, and the fund buys the target (shorting the acquirer in a stock deal) to capture the spread if the deal closes. The payoff is lopsided: many small wins when deals close, a rare large loss when one breaks. It behaves like selling insurance on deal completion, which is fine as long as you price the premium honestly.

Illustrative merger arb outcomes: frequent small spreads earned, occasional large losses.

Macro funds trade entire markets rather than single securities: interest rates, currencies, commodities, equity indices, usually through liquid futures and forwards. Positions express big-picture views, like betting one central bank will cut rates while another hikes. Leverage is standard because the underlying moves are small; sizing and risk management do the heavy lifting. It is the most freewheeling mandate in the industry, and the most dependent on a few big calls a year being right.

Illustrative macro book: directional views expressed long and short across whole markets.

Relative value funds hunt pairs of securities that should trade in a fixed relationship but have drifted apart: two bond issues from the same government, a convertible bond against its underlying stock. Buy the cheap leg, short the rich leg, and earn the convergence regardless of market direction. The edges are tiny, so funds apply leverage to make them worth owning, which is exactly what made LTCM lethal (see below). Quant funds industrialize the same idea, running thousands of small statistical bets by machine and living on the law of large numbers.

Credit funds trade corporate debt across the quality spectrum, from performing high yield down to distressed and defaulted paper. A distressed investor might buy a bond at 60 cents on the dollar, betting the eventual restructuring pays more than that. Some funds buy enough of the debt to control the bankruptcy process itself, converting bonds into ownership of the reorganized company. Returns hinge on legal outcomes and recovery values more than on the equity market's mood.

Long-Term Capital Management, launched in 1994 with two future Nobel laureates on the team, ran relative value trades at roughly 25x leverage on about $4.7B of capital. Its models said the positions were nearly riskless; they were merely usually profitable. When Russia defaulted in August 1998, spreads that had always converged blew apart instead, and LTCM lost roughly $4.4B in a few months. The Federal Reserve corralled 14 banks into a roughly $3.6B recapitalization, not to save the fund but to prevent a fire sale of its positions from cascading through every dealer on the Street. The lesson outlived the fund: leverage converts "eventually right" into "immediately insolvent."

07.5 The price of admission: fee drag

Fees look tiny in any single year and monstrous over a career. A 2% fee does not take 2% of your returns; it takes 2% of your capital every year, earned or not, and the money it removes stops compounding forever. This is the single most consequential piece of arithmetic in the module, so we built a machine for it.

Recall the two fee types. The management fee is charged on assets under management regardless of performance: it pays the rent and the salaries. The performance fee takes a share of profits, classically 20%, usually calculated after the management fee and often subject to a hurdle rate or high-water mark. In private equity the same 20% is called carried interest. Stack them and the net return to the investor looks like this:

Net annual return = Gross return - Management fee - Perf fee % × max(0, Gross return - Management fee)

Fees compound exactly like returns, in reverse. At 8% gross over 30 years, $100K grows to roughly $1.01M before fees. Under 2 and 20 the net return drops to about 4.8% and the final pot to roughly $410K: the investor keeps about a third of the gains their own capital produced. The same investor in a 0.05% index fund keeps roughly 98%. Nothing has to go wrong for this to happen. It is pure arithmetic, which is why the burden of proof always sits on the expensive fund to show its gross returns clear the bar.

This arithmetic is why fee terms, not just returns, are negotiated hard by large allocators, why hedge fund fees have drifted below the classic 2 and 20 toward something like 1.4 and 16 on average, and why the index fund, the cheapest wrapper ever built, keeps winning share. High-fee managers can absolutely be worth it. The tool just shows you exactly how good they have to be.

07.6 How institutions actually decide

Sit in on an investment committee and you discover the least glamorous truth in finance: the big decision is the asset allocation, not the stock picks. Everything else, rebalancing, manager selection, benchmarking, is machinery for staying faithful to that one decision, operated by human beings with careers to protect.

Asset allocation is the whole ballgame

An institution's policy portfolio is its long-run target mix: say 40% global equities, 25% fixed income, 20% private markets, 10% real assets, 5% cash. A famous 1986 study of large US pension plans found that roughly 90% of the variability of a plan's returns over time was explained by its asset allocation policy rather than by security selection or market timing. The exact number has been argued about ever since; the ranking has not. Choose the mix well and the details are survivable. Choose it badly and no manager can save you.

Rebalancing: the discipline machine

Rebalancing means trading back to the policy weights after markets move them. If equities rally from 40% to 46% of the portfolio, the institution sells equities and buys what lagged, typically when weights drift outside preset bands. Notice what this forces: systematically selling what just went up and buying what just went down, which is emotionally miserable and historically sensible. The IPS makes it automatic precisely so that no committee has to feel brave at the bottom of a bear market.

Hiring a manager: the gauntlet

When the allocation calls for outside talent, a mandate goes through a process that can take a year. Consultants call the evaluation framework the four Ps: people, philosophy, process, and performance, roughly in that order of importance, because past performance is the least reliable of the four.

  1. Step 1 · Define
    Define the mandate
    The committee specifies the assignment: asset class, size, benchmark, risk limits. Example: $150M, US small-cap value, benchmarked to a small-cap value index, tracking error under 6%.
  2. Step 2 · Screen
    Screen and issue RFPs
    Databases and consultant ratings cut thousands of funds to a short list, which receives a request for proposal covering strategy, team, capacity, and fees.
  3. Step 3 · Diligence
    Investment due diligence
    On-site visits and interviews probe the four Ps. Does the stated process match the actual portfolio? Who leaves if the star leaves? Is performance skill or one lucky year repeated in the marketing deck?
  4. Step 4 · ODD
    Operational due diligence
    A separate team verifies the boring things: independent administrator, real auditor, segregated custody, compliance staffing. This became non-negotiable after the Madoff fraud surfaced in 2008; his returns were fake, but so was his back office, and ODD would have caught the second part.
  5. Step 5 · Approve
    Committee approval and legal
    The investment committee votes, and lawyers negotiate the investment management agreement: fees, guidelines, termination rights, reporting.
  6. Step 6 · Monitor
    Fund and monitor
    Capital transfers and the quarterly review cycle begins. Underperformance or personnel turnover puts a manager on the watch list; persistent problems end the mandate.

Benchmarks and tracking error

Every mandate is judged against a benchmark: the index the manager is paid to beat. Two statistics frame the evaluation. Tracking error is the volatility of the difference between the portfolio's returns and the benchmark's: it measures how differently the manager behaves, not how well. Active share measures how much the holdings differ from the index. Together they expose a quiet scandal: a fund with low active share and low tracking error is a closet indexer, hugging the benchmark while charging active fees. You now know from section 07.5 exactly why that deal is poor.

Career risk: the invisible hand on the wheel

Here is the agency problem at the heart of institutional investing. The people making these decisions, staff, consultants, committee members, are judged over 1 to 3 year windows, while the portfolios they run have horizons of decades. Underperforming with a famous manager in a conventional portfolio is professionally survivable; underperforming with an unusual choice is not, even if the unusual choice had better odds. So institutions cluster: same benchmarks, same consultants, same manager lists. The old line captures it: nobody ever got fired for hiring the biggest name in the database.

The classic institutional error is judging managers on 1 to 3 year returns. Even genuinely skilled managers spend long stretches behind their benchmarks, so committees that fire the laggard and hire the recent winner end up systematically selling low and buying high. Studies of institutional hiring have found that fired managers often outperform their replacements over the following years. The fix is unglamorous: decide in advance what evidence would justify a firing (people left, process broke, style drifted) and refuse to act on performance alone.

That completes the buy side: who owns the capital, the wrappers it flows through, the funds that swing for alpha, and the fee math that quietly decides outcomes. Next, Module 08 crosses back to where these institutions meet issuers: the capital markets, where stocks and bonds are born and traded.
