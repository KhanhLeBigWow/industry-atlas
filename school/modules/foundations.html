Module 01 · Primer

Foundations of Finance

Finance has a reputation for complexity it does not deserve. Underneath the jargon, the whole industry does 3 simple jobs. Learn those jobs and the rest of this curriculum becomes pattern recognition.

01.1 The 3 jobs of finance

Strip away the towers and the ticker tape, and finance exists to solve 3 problems that every household, company, and government has always had.

JOB 1

Move money through time

You earn money today but need it in retirement. A company needs a factory today but will earn from it for 20 years. Saving, lending, and investing are all machinery for shifting purchasing power between your present self and your future self.

JOB 2

Pool and allocate capital

No single household can fund a semiconductor plant. Finance collects small pools of savings into large ones, then competes to route them toward the projects expected to earn the best return. When it works, good ideas get funded and bad ones starve.

JOB 3

Transfer and price risk

A farmer cannot control grain prices; an airline cannot control fuel. Insurance, derivatives, and even plain equity let risk move from people who cannot bear it to people paid to bear it. The price of that transfer is one of the most useful numbers in the economy.

Every product you will meet in later modules is a combination of these 3 jobs. A share of stock moves money through time (you invest now, get dividends later), pools capital (thousands of shareholders fund one company), and prices risk (the share price tells you what the market charges for that company's uncertainty). A mortgage, an IPO, a leveraged buyout: same 3 ingredients, different recipes.

When a financial product seems baffling, ask: whose money is moving through time, whose capital is being pooled, and who is being paid to hold which risk? If you cannot answer any of the 3, either you do not understand the product yet, or nobody does. Both happen.

01.2 Two routes from saver to spender

Money gets from the people who have it to the people who can use it by exactly 2 routes: through an intermediary's balance sheet, or directly through a market. The entire industry is variations on these 2 pipes.

Intermediated vs direct finance

Same saver, same borrower, 2 different plumbing systems.

Rates illustrative. The dashed border matters: in direct finance, the intermediary arranges the flow but does not hold the loan.

In intermediated finance, a bank stands in the middle and takes the risk onto its own balance sheet. Your deposit is a loan to the bank; the bank's loan to a business is the bank's problem if it sours. You get safety and liquidity; the bank keeps the spread as payment for holding the risk and doing the credit work.

In direct (market) finance, the saver holds the borrower's IOU directly, as a bond or a share. Investment banks, exchanges, and funds make this route work, but they mostly arrange, package, and trade rather than hold. Risk sits with the end investor, and so does more of the return.

The US economy leans on markets more than almost anywhere else; Europe and Asia historically lean on banks. That single difference explains a lot about where investment banking, private credit, and giant bond markets flourish.

The 2008 financial crisis was, at its core, a failure of both pipes at once. Banks had loaded balance sheets with mortgage risk they had misjudged, and markets had distributed the same risk through securities so opaque that when trust broke, buyers simply vanished. Lending froze not because savings disappeared, but because the machinery connecting savers to borrowers stopped being trusted. Roughly $2 trillion of global bank writedowns later, the lesson stands: finance runs on confidence in the plumbing.

01.3 The instruments: debt, equity, and everything between

Every security is a claim on someone's future cash flows. What varies is where you stand in line when those cash flows arrive, and what you were promised.

Debt is a promise: fixed payments on fixed dates, and legal remedies if they do not show up. Lenders get paid first but their upside is capped at the interest they were promised. Equity is ownership of whatever is left after everyone else is paid: unlimited upside, last in line, no promises at all. Hybrids like preferred stock and convertible bonds mix the two, trading some safety for some upside or vice versa.

The capital structure: who gets paid first

One company's capital, stacked by priority of claim. Risk and expected return rise as you move down.

In a bankruptcy, value pours from the top: each layer must be filled in full before a dollar reaches the next. Equity often gets zero, which is exactly the deal equity signed up for.

Newcomers assume higher yield means a better investment. Read the stack again: yield is the price of risk, not a gift. A bond paying 12% when Treasuries pay 4% is telling you, loudly, that repayment is in doubt. In finance, unusually generous offers are warnings wearing a costume.

01.4 Time value of money: the one formula that runs everything

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year, because today's dollar can be invested and grow. That single sentence, made precise, is the engine inside every valuation, every loan, and every retirement plan.

Two directions, one machine. Compounding pushes money forward through time: invest PV today at rate r for n years and it becomes the future value. Discounting pulls money backward: a payment of FV due in n years is worth less today, and the discount rate r says how much less.

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)^n with compounding m times per year: FV = PV × (1 + r/m)^(m×n)

The discount rate is where finance hides all its judgment. It bundles the risk-free alternative (what Treasuries pay), expected inflation, and a premium for the risk that the payment never arrives. Riskier promise, higher r, lower present value. Module 04 spends a whole section choosing r; for now, feel how violently it matters:

You are promised $1,000,000 in 10 years. What is that promise worth today?

At 4% (a safe promisor): $1,000,000 ÷ 1.04^10 = $675,564.
At 8% (a normal business): $1,000,000 ÷ 1.08^10 = $463,193.
At 12% (a shaky startup): $1,000,000 ÷ 1.12^10 = $321,973.

Same promise, half the value, purely from doubt. When someone argues about a discount rate, they are arguing about trust, dressed in math.

01.5 Risk and return: the tradeoff nobody escapes

Higher expected returns are compensation for enduring bigger swings and scarier worst cases. The market lets you pick your seat on that spectrum, but it never lets you skip the ride.

The risk-return menu

Illustrative shapes, not forecasts. Venture's dot hides brutal dispersion: the average is dragged up by a few huge winners while most individual bets lose money.

Volatility is the polite word for the experience of watching your investment fall 30% in a bad year. The reason stocks out-earn bonds over decades is precisely that they can do that to you, and sometimes do. If an asset reliably paid stock-like returns with T-bill-like calm, everyone would buy it, its price would rise, and its future return would fall until the tradeoff reappeared. Markets grind free lunches out of existence.

The one honest discount on the price of risk is diversification. Individual risks that do not move together partially cancel: one airline's strike does not ground the whole portfolio. Spreading capital across many imperfectly-correlated assets lowers the swing without lowering the expected return by the same amount. It is the closest thing finance has to a free lunch, which is why every institution in Module 07 is built around it.

Formally, only systematic risk (the part correlated with the whole market, measured by beta) should earn a premium, because the diversifiable part can be washed out for free. That idea, the Capital Asset Pricing Model, resurfaces in Module 04 as the standard way to estimate the cost of equity. It is imperfect and everyone knows it; it also runs the world's discount rates anyway.

01.6 Interest rates: the gravity of the system

Central bank policy rates are the price of money itself. Move that one price and every other asset price on Earth must renegotiate its position.

Remember the discounting math from 01.4: present value = future cash ÷ (1 + r)^n. The r in that formula starts from the risk-free rate. When the central bank raises rates, r rises for everything, and the present value of every stream of future cash falls, mechanically. Assets whose cash flows sit far in the future (growth stocks, 30-year bonds, speculative ventures) get hit hardest, because the exponent n amplifies the change.

When rates rise sharply...Why
Long bonds fall hardOld fixed coupons look stingy next to new ones; price adjusts down (Module 08 has a tool for exactly this)
Growth stocks fall harder than value stocksTheir cash flows live decades out, where discounting bites deepest
Deals and IPOs slow downDebt costs more, valuations compress, sellers cling to yesterday's prices
Cash stops being trashSitting in T-bills finally pays; risk-taking must clear a higher bar
Banks feel both sidesLending margins can widen while bond portfolios show losses

In 2022, the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate from roughly 0% to over 4% inside a year to fight inflation, one of the fastest climbs on record. The result was a textbook demonstration: US aggregate bond indices had their worst year in decades (down roughly 13%), the Nasdaq fell about 33%, IPO volume collapsed by more than 90% from the prior year's peak, and M&A slowed sharply. No scandal, no crash in earnings. Just gravity being switched back on.

This is why the entire industry watches central banks with the intensity of sailors watching weather. Rates set the tide every boat floats on: valuation multiples, deal activity, fund returns, mortgage payments.
